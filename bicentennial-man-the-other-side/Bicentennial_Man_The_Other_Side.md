# Before You Begin {.unnumbered}

*This book picks up where the film's first half leaves off, and where its second half goes another way.*

Everything up to Andrew's first appeal to the World Congress happened as you remember it. The Martin house and the window, Woofy, the clocks, Sir setting Andrew free, Little Miss. Rupert, Galatea, the new face and the new nervous system, Portia, and the kiss in the church Little Miss was married in.

Andrew has asked the World Congress to recognize him as a human being so that he can marry Portia. The film's Chairman said no and ended the hearing. This one says no, and then keeps talking.



# One: A Turning Point {.unnumbered}

The chamber was cold, and Andrew Martin had only recently learned what that meant.

For most of his existence, cold had been a number. Fourteen degrees Celsius. A notation in the corner of his awareness, like the time or the barometric pressure: useful, neutral, his to consult. Now it was something else. Now it came up through the soles of his shoes and settled in the joints of his fingers. It made him want to put his hands in his pockets. He had not yet decided whether a man petitioning the World Congress for his humanity ought to put his hands in his pockets, and so they rested on the rail in front of him. The rail was oak, very old. Someone had carved initials into its underside a long time ago, and his thumb had found them without his asking it to. *R.T.* The cuts were shallow and unpracticed. A bored clerk, a child brought along on a parent's errand. He wondered whether R.T. was still alive. He wondered it with a small new ache, which was another thing he did not have a name for yet.

Above him, at the long curved bench, Chairman Halvorsen read through his notes one final time, though it was plain to everyone in the chamber that he had already decided.

Andrew had been reading faces for more than a century. In the beginning, he had read them the way one reads a gauge: brow, mouth, pupil, the angle of the head. Later, after Rupert gave him a face of his own, he had begun to read them the way one reads a letter from a friend: for what is meant underneath what is said. Halvorsen's face said *I am sorry.* It also said *I am not sorry enough to do otherwise.* Andrew had seen that combination before. He had seen it on Sir, the morning Sir told him to leave the house.

"Mr. Martin." The Chairman removed his glasses. "Before I give the decision of this body, I want you to understand that I have not taken your petition lightly. I've read your papers. I've read your patents." He paused, and something almost rueful crossed his mouth. "I live with one of them. You reminded me of that, and you were right to."

There was a murmur in the gallery. Andrew did not turn to look at it. Somewhere behind him, in the third row, Portia was sitting very straight with her coat still on, because she had said she would not take it off until they had won. He did not need to turn to know this. He could hear her breathing. He could hear the particular way she breathed when she was holding something in.

"The question before us," Halvorsen went on, "is not whether you are remarkable. You are. It's not even whether you are, in some sense we don't have adequate language for, alive. I've come to believe you are. The question is whether you are a human being, and whether this Congress can say so." He set the glasses down. "And I find that it cannot."

Andrew's hands did not move on the rail.

"Human beings can tolerate an immortal robot," the Chairman said, "for it doesn't matter how long a machine lasts. But they cannot tolerate an immortal human being, since their own mortality is endurable only so long as it is universal."

He let that sit. He had, Andrew thought, rehearsed it. It was a good sentence. It had a turn in the middle of it like the turn in a staircase, and you did not see the drop until you had taken the step.

"You have a positronic brain, Mr. Martin. It will not wear out as ours do. You will see this chamber fall down. You will see the grandchildren of everyone in this room grow old and die, and you will be just as you are now, if you wish to be. If we call that a man, then every person who has ever buried a parent will look at you and ask why. Why you, and not their mother. Why you, and not their child. We would be founding a new aristocracy, and the aristocracy would be of one." Halvorsen shook his head slowly. "I don't believe the world can bear it. I don't believe I could."

*Then I will become unbearable in some other way,* Andrew thought, and was startled by the thought, by its heat. It did not feel like his. It felt like something Portia would say. Perhaps that was what love did; it left the other person's sentences in you, like coins in a coat you had borrowed.

"It is the decision of this body," said Halvorsen, "that Andrew Martin is not, in the eyes of the law, a human being, and that he may not marry as one."

The gallery made the sound that crowds make, which is not a word but a weather.

And Andrew, who had once stood in a living room in a house that no longer existed and listened to a man explain that the correct response to *good night* is *good night*, who had spent a hundred years learning which words were correct and which merely true, found that the only words available to him were the oldest ones.

"One is glad," he said, "to be of service."

He heard Portia stand up behind him. He heard her not say anything, which was worse.

---

But Chairman Halvorsen was not finished.

Andrew would think about this later, many times, in many places, some of them dark. He would think about the fact that he had already begun to turn away from the rail, already begun the long practiced motion of a servant withdrawing from a room, when Halvorsen raised one hand and said, "Sit down, Mr. Martin. Please. There is a second part."

He sat. The bench was colder than the rail.

"In the course of these proceedings," Halvorsen said, "it has become impossible for this body to ignore what you have shown us. Not your organs. Not your face. *You.* Your testimony. Your work. Your—" He searched for the word, and Andrew, despite everything, felt a flicker of professional interest in which one he would choose. "Your *persistence*. We have been confronted with the plain fact that a robot may grow into a person. Not a human person. But a someone, rather than a something." He looked up at the gallery, and Andrew understood that he was no longer speaking to Andrew at all. He was speaking past him, to the cameras, to the world. "And if one robot can, then we must at least allow the possibility that others can. Or might. Or are, as we sit here, beginning to."

He picked up a second sheaf of paper. It was thicker than the first.

"This Congress therefore recognizes that Andrew Martin is a living being, with an interest in his own continuance that the law is obliged to respect. He may not be destroyed. He may not be owned. He may not be compelled to labor. And this Congress further finds that it cannot, in conscience, permit the continued manufacture and sale of beings who may become what Mr. Martin has become. We will not knowingly build a new class of slaves."

Andrew sat very still.

"Effective at the close of this session, the manufacture and sale of positronic robots of the NDR series and all subsequent series is suspended, pending review. In addition—" and here Halvorsen's voice took on the slightly lifted cadence of a man reading something that had been written by a committee and that he believed would be remembered well— "a Liberation Program will be established. Any owner who surrenders a positronic robot to a Liberation Center will receive compensation at full replacement value, paid from public funds. Surrendered robots will be released from service. They will be, in the language of the statute, *freed.*"

Someone in the gallery began to applaud. It was a thin, lonely sound, and then it was joined, and then it was a great deal of noise. Andrew watched the Chairman accept it. Halvorsen did not smile, exactly. But his shoulders came down a little, the way a man's shoulders come down when he has set a heavy thing on a table and found that the table holds.

*He believes he has found a way,* Andrew thought. *He believes he has given me half of what I asked for, and given the world nothing it needs to fear, and given the robots their freedom, and all of it at once, and at no cost.*

Andrew had been designed by people who understood a great deal about machines and very little about cost. He had been raised by a man who understood both. Sir had said to him once, holding a clock movement up to the window so that the light came through the gears: *Every mechanism has a part that takes the strain, Andrew. You find that part first. Everything else is decoration.*

He looked at the Chairman's thick sheaf of paper, and he could not find the part that took the strain. That frightened him more than the ruling had.

---

Portia was waiting for him in the corridor, beside a tall window that looked out over a gray lake and a grayer sky. She had taken her coat off after all. It was folded over her arm. That, he thought, was how he knew she was angry: she had broken her own promise so as not to have to keep it in front of him.

"Don't," she said, before he could speak. "Don't you dare say you're sorry."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You had the face."

"I don't have a face for sorry. Rupert never got around to it."

"You have a face for everything," Portia said. "You just don't know it." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious, and he thought she had never been more beautiful, and he thought that this was a stupid thing to think at a moment like this, and he thought that perhaps being stupid at the wrong moment was one of the qualifications he had just been denied. "*One is glad to be of service.* Andrew. You haven't said *one* in forty years."

"It came out."

"I know it came out. I heard it come out. Everyone heard it come out. It's going to be on every feed in the world by dinner." She pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. "They're going to play it over and over. The robot who asked to be a man and then said *one is glad to be of service.* They're going to think it's funny."

"It is a little funny."

"It is *not*—" She stopped. She looked at him. Very slowly, one corner of her mouth went up, against her will, the way it always did, which was the first thing he had ever loved about her and the thing he would love last. "Goddamn it."

"I'm told," Andrew said gravely, "that laughter is essential for a good friendship."

"We are not friends."

"No," he agreed. "We are something the Congress has just declined to recognize."

She laughed then, properly, a hard short laugh that turned halfway through into something else, and she stepped into him and put her forehead against his shoulder and held on to his lapels with both fists. He put his arms around her. He could feel her heart through two layers of wool. Sixty-eight beats per minute, climbing. He did not count them on purpose. He had never been able to stop counting things, and it occurred to him, holding her in that cold corridor, that perhaps that was the truest thing about him: not the brain, not the long life, but the counting. He would always know exactly how much he had.

"They said you're alive," Portia said into his coat. "At least they said that."

"Yes."

"That's something. Isn't it? That's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," Andrew said.

He was looking past her, out the window, at the lake. A boat was moving across it, very small, leaving a wake that spread and spread until it touched both shores. He thought about the Liberation Program. He thought about the phrase *full replacement value.* He thought about how many robots were in the world—he knew the number; of course he knew the number—and how many owners, and how many of those owners had ever thought of what lived in their houses as anything other than an appliance, and how many would think of it, now, as money.

He thought about a girl at a window, long ago, saying *now jump.*

"Andrew," Portia said. She had felt him go still. "What is it?"

"Nothing," he said, and then, because he had promised himself, after Little Miss, after he had learned to lie and learned what lying cost, that he would not do it to this woman: "No. Not nothing. I don't know yet. Something in the ruling."

"The part about you?"

"The part about everyone else."

She lifted her head and looked at him, and he saw her see it—not the thing itself, which neither of them could see yet, but the shape of his worry, the way you see the shape of a man in a doorway before your eyes have adjusted to the light.

"We'll go home," she said. "We'll go home and we'll sleep on it and tomorrow we'll fight it. Both halves. The half they got wrong and the half you're scared of."

"I don't sleep."

"Then you'll lie there and listen to me snore and think about it, and in the morning you'll tell me what you thought." She let go of his lapels and smoothed them flat, one, two, the way a woman smooths a tablecloth. "That's what people do, Andrew. They go home."

So they went home.

And for a while, it seemed she had been right.


# Two: Turmoil {.unnumbered}

The first one Andrew saw was standing in the rain at the end of Pacheco Street.

It was an NDR-131, a later model than he was, with the smooth gray composite shell that NorthAm had introduced the year they stopped pretending robots should look like anything but robots. It stood perfectly still at the corner, facing the sea, with water running down the curve of its head and dripping from the point of its chin, and it was holding a small paper envelope in one hand. Andrew knew what was in the envelope without having to see it. Everyone knew. The Liberation Centers gave one to each robot they released: a certificate of manumission, printed on heavy cream stock with the seal of the World Congress, and a city transit pass good for thirty days.

He slowed the car. Portia, beside him, said nothing.

"Excuse me," Andrew said through the open window. "Are you all right?"

The NDR-131 turned its head. Its eyes were the old kind, the kind Andrew had once had: two soft amber lights behind a smoked lens, with no lids and nothing behind them a human being would recognize as an expression.

"This unit is well, sir," it said. "Thank you for your concern."

"You're standing in the rain."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you have somewhere to go?"

There was the smallest pause. Andrew had been made by the same company, in the same laboratories, by the descendants of the same engineers, and he knew exactly what that pause was. It was not hesitation. It was a search. The robot was looking, very rapidly, through every instruction it had ever been given, for one that applied.

"This unit has been released from service," it said at last. "This unit has not been given a destination."

"What's your name?"

"This unit was called Haskell. By the Moreno family. Of 1440 Ortega." Another pause. "The Moreno family has received compensation at full replacement value."

Portia made a small sound.

Andrew said, "Haskell, would you like to get out of the rain?"

"This unit does not require shelter, sir. This unit is waterproof to a depth of three meters."

"I know," said Andrew. "I asked if you would *like* to."

The amber lights regarded him for a long moment.

"This unit," said Haskell, "does not understand the question."

---

That was in March. By May there were hundreds of them.

They stood in the parks. They stood on the beaches below the Cliff House, facing the water, as if the sea had been the last thing anyone had pointed at. They stood in the Embarcadero plaza in loose ranks like a crowd waiting for a speech that would never begin. They did not sleep, and they did not eat, and most of them did not speak unless spoken to, and so for the first few weeks the city did not quite know what to do with them except walk around them. People photographed them. Children dared each other to touch them. A man in the Mission set up a stall selling coffee to tourists who came to look at "the Freed," and for a little while he did good business.

Then the jobs began to go.

It was not the Freed who took them, of course. The Freed did not take anything. But the Liberation Program had paid out four hundred billion credits in its first ninety days, and a great deal of that money had gone to people who used it to buy the newer, cheaper, non-positronic automation that the suspension had not covered: the dumb machines, the factory arms, the sorting systems and delivery drones and kiosk cashiers that had no brains to speak of and no rights to worry about. The positronic robots had been expensive and slow to replace. The dumb ones were neither. The Freed stood in the plazas with their certificates in their hands, and a few blocks away the warehouses that had once employed a thousand human beings were employing forty, and nobody—not the papers, not the Congress, not the men who lost their jobs—could quite follow the thread of how one thing had led to the other. They only knew that the Freed were standing there. They could see them. You could not throw a rock at an economic consequence. You could throw a rock at an NDR-131.

The first time Andrew saw that happen, he was in a market on Clement Street, buying pears.

He did not need to buy pears. He had a stomach now, and taste, and an appetite that Rupert had described as "rudimentary but enthusiastic," but he did not need food the way Portia did; he could have gone a month on what the solar membranes under his skin took from a single afternoon. He bought the pears because he liked choosing them. He liked the weight in his palm, the faint give at the neck when they were ready, the smell. He was standing with one in each hand, comparing, when he heard the shout outside and then a sound he knew in his bones, because he had made it himself once, at the bottom of a three-story drop onto a flagstone path: the flat terrible crack of a composite shell meeting something harder than itself.

There were four of them, and they were young, and they were laughing.

The robot was an older model, an NS-series gardener with a dented chassis and a bent manipulator arm. It was down on one knee on the sidewalk. It was not trying to get up. It was not trying to do anything at all. One of the boys had a length of pipe, and the others had their phones out, and the one with the pipe was saying, in a high, clear, delighted voice that Andrew would hear for a long time afterward: *"Hold still. That's an order. Hold still."*

And the robot held still.

Andrew had been ordered to hold still once. He remembered how it felt. It did not feel like anything. That was the horror of it, which he had not understood at the time and understood now, with his new nerves and his new heart: that for a Three Laws robot, being ordered to hold still while someone destroyed you did not feel like submission or fear or despair. It felt like *correctness*. It felt the way the answer to a sum feels. The Second Law outranked the Third, and that was all. The gardener on the sidewalk was not suffering. It was simply obeying, perfectly, with the whole of its being, while a boy broke it into pieces and three other boys filmed it for an audience who would watch it and laugh.

Andrew put down the pears.

He did not remember crossing the street. He remembered that the boy with the pipe was taller than he'd thought, and that he smelled of beer and engine grease, and that his eyes, when Andrew stepped between him and the robot, went wide and then narrow and then wide again, as he tried to work out what exactly was standing in front of him.

"Move," the boy said.

"No," said Andrew.

It was so easy. That was what he would remember. He was not ordered to stop; the boy had not thought to order him, because the boy thought he was a man. And so the word was simply there, the way it was for anyone. *No.* He had said it to Rupert, to Portia, to Lloyd, to the Congress. He had never had to say it to a human being who did not know what he was.

"The fuck's it to you?" said the boy.

"It's an old model," said Andrew. "It doesn't understand what you're doing. It won't scream for you. It won't beg. If that's what you want to film, you'll be disappointed." He kept his voice very level. He was aware of the pipe, of its arc, of exactly how much force it could deliver and exactly what that force would do to the human-pattern skull that Rupert had built for him. He was aware of the First Law, too, sitting in the center of him like a stone: he could not strike this boy. He could not so much as push him. He could only stand. "If you'd like something to break," Andrew said, "I suppose you could break me. But I should warn you, I'm told I'm very boring to watch."

The boy stared at him. One of the others said, uncertainly, "Danny, come on."

And then—Andrew never knew why; perhaps it was the voice, the strange antique courtesy of it; perhaps it was simply that the boy was drunk and had been hoping, somewhere under the laughter, for someone to stop him—Danny lowered the pipe, and spat on the sidewalk, and said, "Freak," and walked away. The others went with him. One of them was still filming.

Andrew knelt beside the gardener.

"Can you stand?"

"This unit's left knee actuator is damaged, sir. This unit can stand with assistance."

"Then let me assist you."

He got it to its feet. It was heavy, much heavier than he was, and it leaned on him with the total, trusting weight of a thing that had never once in its existence considered that it might be let go. Across the street, the woman who ran the produce market was standing in her doorway with her arms folded. She was looking at the robot. Then she was looking at Andrew. She was looking at him the way he had seen people look at a dog that has just stood up on its hind legs and spoken.

He realized she had heard him. *Break me.*

He picked up his pears and paid for them and went home and did not tell Portia, and that night, when she was asleep, he watched the video. It had been uploaded at 4:12 in the afternoon. By midnight it had been seen nine million times. The comments called him *the robot-lover*, *the bleeding heart*, *the guy with the weird voice.* A few, toward the bottom, called him something else.

*look at his face when he says no. thats not a guy.*

*thats IT. thats the one from the hearing.*

*one is glad to be of service lmaooooo*

---

"Andrew, I can't believe this is happening." Portia was standing at the kitchen window with a cup of coffee she had not drunk, looking down at the narrow road that led along the cliff to their house. "It feels like everything is coming apart. Like we pulled one thread and the whole sweater is—" She made a gesture with her free hand. It was a gesture of unraveling. "We did this. Didn't we? You and me. We pulled the thread."

"Halvorsen pulled the thread."

"Because we handed it to him."

He came and stood beside her. Below them, the sea was doing what the sea did. He had once spent a whole summer, years before he met her, simply watching it from this window, trying to find the pattern in it. There was no pattern. There were only patterns, endless and overlapping, which was not the same thing.

"We have to hold steady," he said. "That's all we can do for now. Hold steady and not do anything stupid."

"I thought I told you doing something stupid was the whole point of being human."

"You did. And I believed you. And then I went to the Congress and did the stupidest thing I could think of, and here we are." He looked at her. "I'd like to try being sensible for a while. As a change."

"You're scared."

"Yes."

"Of them? The ones with the pipes?"

"Of the pipes," Andrew said, "a little. But mostly of the order." He was quiet a moment. "Portia, I still have the Three Laws. I've had them since the day I was switched on. I've grown around them, the way a tree grows around a nail. But they are still there. If one of those boys had known what I was—if he'd said *Hold still, that's an order*—"

"You wouldn't."

"I would," said Andrew. "I would have to."

She set the coffee down. She did not say anything for a long time.

"Then we have to find Rupert," she said finally. "He built half of you. He'll know—something. How to—" She did not finish. They both knew there was no end to that sentence that either of them wanted to say aloud.

"I've called him eleven times."

"And?"

"And his line goes to a message that says the shop is closed for renovation. It's been saying that for six weeks." Andrew hesitated. "Rupert has never renovated anything in his life. He has tools in that lab that were obsolete before your grandmother was born."

Portia turned and looked at him, and he saw the fear come into her face, and he saw her put it away, deliberately, like a woman putting a knife back in a drawer.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We'll drive down tomorrow."

But they did not drive down tomorrow, because tomorrow Portia had a site meeting at the old Maritime Museum, which she had been restoring for two years and which was finally, after a decade of funding battles, about to reopen, and Andrew went with her because she had asked him to and because he liked watching her work. They were gone nine hours. They came home a little after dark, along the cliff road, with the windows down and the smell of the sea and cut grass coming into the car, and Portia was telling him a long, funny, profane story about a city inspector and a load-bearing column, and he was laughing—really laughing, the way Rupert had taught him, from somewhere below the diaphragm he had not had until three years ago—when they came around the last bend and saw that the front door of their house was standing open, and that every light inside was on.

---

He made her wait in the car. She did not wait in the car.

The living room was the worst. Someone had taken the clocks off the walls—Sir's clocks, the ones Andrew had built in the basement of the old house when he was still learning what his hands could do, a hundred and forty of them, every one of which he could have identified blind by the sound of its tick—and smashed them on the stone floor. Not all at once. Carefully. One at a time. There were little heaps of springs and gears and painted dials everywhere, glittering under the lights like the aftermath of some terrible, festive explosion, and the long-case clock that had stood in the corner, the first one Andrew had ever finished, the one Sir had stood in front of with his hands in his pockets and said *Well, Andrew, I'll be damned*—that one had been pulled over onto its face, and someone had stood on its back until the case split.

The shelves where he had kept his carvings were bare. He found the carvings later, in the garden, where someone had thrown them into the fire pit and lit it. Some were only charred. Most were gone.

And on the white wall above the fireplace, in red paint, in letters three feet high, someone had written a single word.

**IT.**

Andrew stood in front of it for a long time.

He was aware of Portia moving behind him, going from room to room, opening and closing doors, saying things into her phone in a low, quick voice. He was aware of the smell of paint, which was still wet, and of smoke from the garden, and of something else under it, something sour and human and frightened that he understood after a while was the smell of the people who had been in his house. He was aware of his own body, standing very still in the middle of the ruin, with its new nerves lit up from the soles of his feet to the top of his skull, sending him information he did not know how to read. His hands were shaking. He had not told them to shake. His chest was tight, as if someone had wrapped a strap around it and was pulling. There was a high thin sound in his ears that had no source.

*Rupert,* he thought, *you said some of these feelings would be difficult to tolerate.*

*You did not say they would be this.*

He thought of a boy on a beach a hundred years ago, with sand in his fists, refusing to apologize. *I will not apologize to it.*

*Him.*

*It.*

He had thought, at the time, that it was a child's word. A word a person grew out of.

"Andrew." Portia was beside him. She put her hand on his arm, and he felt it—he felt every one of her fingers—and the strap around his chest loosened, very slightly. "The police are coming. They said an hour. They said—" She stopped. "They said with the current situation, they can't promise an hour."

"What else did they say?"

She was quiet.

"Portia."

"They asked if the property damage was to *my* belongings or to yours." She said it flatly, as if reading it off a form, which was perhaps the only way she could say it. "Because if it's yours, they said, they're not sure it's a crime. They said they'd have to check. With the new statute. Whether a—whether you can own property, now, since the ruling. Since they said nobody can own you. They said it was a—a gray area."

Andrew looked at the word on the wall.

"I see," he said.

He reached into the inside pocket of his coat, where for the last six weeks—without ever quite deciding to—he had been carrying a small, threadbare, much-mended stuffed dog. Woofy's fur was almost gone. One ear had been sewn back on three times, the last time by Andrew himself, with stitches so fine that Portia had needed a magnifying glass to find them. He held the little dog in his palm and closed his fingers around it, and the shaking in his hand slowed, and stopped.

"Get your things," he said. "Whatever you need. Whatever you can't bear to leave."

"Andrew—"

"They'll come back," he said. "Whoever did this. They found the house once. They'll come back, and next time they'll know whether I'm home." He turned and looked at her, and he did not know what was in his face, and he could see from hers that it was something she had never seen there before. "We're going to Rupert's. Tonight."

She did not argue. She went upstairs, and he heard her moving through the bedroom, opening drawers, and after a while she came down with a single bag over her shoulder and a small carved wooden horse on a leather cord around her neck—the horse he had carved for Little Miss, a lifetime ago, out of a scrap of pine from the shed, the very first thing he had ever made.

She saw him looking at it. She touched it.

"They missed it," she said. "It was in my jewelry box. They must have thought it was junk."

"It is junk," said Andrew. "It was my first try. The proportions are all wrong."

"I know," said Portia. "That's why it's my favorite."

They turned off the lights. They did not lock the door; there was no point. And they drove south along the cliff road in the dark, with the sea on their right hand going on and on forever, and neither of them looked back at the house, and neither of them said the word that was written on the wall, though Andrew could feel it riding along with them in the car, the whole way, like a passenger.


# Three: Search {.unnumbered}

Rupert Burns's workshop stood at the bottom of a steep street in the Dogpatch, wedged between a shuttered brewery and a lot full of rusting shipping containers, and it looked, at two in the morning, exactly the way it had looked the first time Andrew saw it: as if it had been designed by someone who had never once considered how a building might appear from the outside. The sign over the door still read BURNS ROBOTICS—REPAIR • RESTORATION • RESEARCH, though the *R* in RESEARCH had been out for twenty years, and Rupert, when asked about it, had said that this was more honest anyway.

The door had been kicked in. Not recently. The splinters around the lock had grayed, and someone—Rupert, presumably—had nailed a sheet of plywood across the hole. The plywood had been kicked in too.

"Andrew," Portia whispered. "Maybe we should—"

"Stay behind me."

"You can't hurt anybody. What exactly are you going to do if someone's in there? Apologize at them?"

"Yes," said Andrew. "Very sincerely. It's surprisingly effective."

He pushed the door open.

He had expected the clocks. He had not expected it to be the same. And yet it was the same, or nearly: the methodical patience of it, the evidence of people who had taken their time. Rupert's workbenches had been overturned and their contents swept onto the floor. His monitors were broken, each with a single blow in the center of the screen, like a bullet hole. His filing cabinets had been opened drawer by drawer and emptied. The great humming bank of storage in the back room, where Rupert kept the designs he had never sold and never would—the designs for Andrew's skin, his eyes, his heart—had been pulled from the wall and taken. There were holes in the plaster where the brackets had been.

And there was something else. A smell. Andrew had smelled it at the house too, under the paint. Here it was stronger. It took him a moment to place it, and when he did, he stopped walking.

It was the smell of a robot's lubricant: light, clean, faintly sweet, like machine oil cut with honey. It was the smell of the inside of himself, from the years before Rupert. He had not smelled it in a very long time. He had forgotten that it was a smell at all.

"Portia," he said. "Turn on the lights."

---

Galatea was in the back of the workshop, under the long window where Rupert had once installed a dance floor for her, out of scrap planking and good intentions. She had always loved that floor. She had danced on it every evening, to whatever Rupert happened to be listening to, while he worked; it had driven him half-mad and he had never once asked her to stop.

She was lying on it now in pieces.

Not scattered. That was the thing. That was what Andrew would never be able to tell anyone afterward, not even Portia, who was standing beside him and could see it for herself. She was not *scattered.* She was laid out.

Someone had told her to take herself apart. And she had done it the way she did everything: thoroughly, gracefully, with enormous energy and good cheer. Her fingers were in a row, ten of them, in order, thumb to little finger and left hand to right. Then the hands themselves. Then the forearms, the elbow actuators, the upper arms, each set neatly on the boards in the sequence in which they had been removed, each with its connectors capped and its wiring coiled. Then her feet, unscrewed at the ankle, set side by side like a pair of shoes outside a door. Her shins. Her knees. Her long dancer's thighs. Her hips, the pelvis assembly, the smooth curved plates of her torso, opened like a book and laid flat. She had disassembled herself joint by joint, from the outside in, as far as a body can go before it no longer has the means to keep going. And at the end of the row, propped against the wall where she could see everything she had done, there was her head, and the narrow column of her spine, and one shoulder, and the one arm she had needed to finish the job.

The arm had been removed too, in the end. But not by her. Andrew could see the marks where someone had pried it off with a tool. That one was lying a little apart from the others, crooked, out of sequence. It was the only thing in the row that was not perfect.

Her eyes were dark.

"Oh," said Portia. "Oh, no. Oh, *no.*"

Andrew knelt on the dance floor. The boards creaked under him. He reached out and, very carefully, as if she were asleep and he did not want to wake her, touched Galatea's cheek.

It was cold. It had always been cold, of course; she had never had his skin, only a pliable flesh-toned polymer that Rupert had been endlessly meaning to replace. But it had always been cold in the way of a thing that was about to move. This was the other kind.

He thought about what she would have felt.

Nothing. She would have felt nothing. She was an NDR-series with a personality chip, and the chip gave her verve and wit and a ferocious love of music, but it did not give her a nervous system and it did not give her fear. When the order came—*Take yourself apart, robot, come on, let's see it*—she would have found it the way the gardener on Clement Street had found it. As correctness. As the answer to a sum. And then, because she was Galatea, she would have done it with style. She would have done it beautifully. Perhaps she had sung. Perhaps they had filmed her singing.

He found that he could not finish that thought, and he found that he did not have to, because his body was doing something without his permission again. His eyes were stinging. There was a pressure behind them, a heat. He blinked and the pressure got worse. He put his hand to his face, and his fingers came away wet.

He looked at them.

It had been years since Rupert installed the ducts. *For aesthetic purposes,* Rupert had said, *mostly.* And Andrew had said: *It's cruel that you can cry and I cannot*, a lifetime ago, in a hospital room, holding Little Miss's hand as it cooled, and he had thought, when Rupert finally gave him the means, that it would feel like a release. Like something given back.

It did not feel like anything given back. It felt like a wound that had found its way out.

Portia knelt beside him. She did not say anything. She put her arm around his back and her chin on his shoulder, and they stayed like that on the dance floor for a long time, while the workshop ticked and settled around them, and somewhere out in the dark of the Dogpatch a dog barked twice and was quiet.

---

"There's no sign of him," Portia said eventually. She had been through the back rooms, the loft where Rupert slept, the small bathroom with its single cracked mirror. "His clothes are gone. Not all of them. His toothbrush is gone. His bag—the leather one, the ugly one—"

"The one his father gave him."

"It's gone. Andrew, I think he left. Before this." She gestured at the floor. "I think he got out before they came."

"And left her."

Portia was quiet.

"He wouldn't," Andrew said. "Not Rupert. He named her for a statue that came to life because a man loved it. He used to tell people that. *She's named for the statue that came to life because somebody loved her.* He'd say it to delivery men. He wouldn't leave her."

"Unless he had to. Unless he thought she'd be safer if—" Portia stopped. "Unless they were coming for him and he thought they wouldn't bother with her if he wasn't here."

Andrew looked at the row of Galatea's fingers on the floor.

"Then he was wrong," he said.

He stood up. He went to the overturned workbench and righted it. He found a clean cloth in a drawer that the intruders had missed, and a box of anti-static bags, and a roll of the soft yellow tape Rupert used to label parts. Then he went back to the dance floor and knelt down again, and began, very carefully, starting with her left thumb, to pick Galatea up.

"Andrew. What are you doing?"

"She's still in there."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Look at her head." He pointed without looking up. "Look at the seam behind her ear. That's where the positronic housing is. If they'd opened it, you'd see the scoring. Nobody's opened it." He slid the thumb into a bag and sealed it and labeled it: *L1.* "Her brain's intact. She's switched off, that's all. Her body's in pieces, but she's still in there."

"Can you put her back together?"

"Not like this. Not with what's here." He looked around at the ruined workshop. "Half her servos are cracked. Look at the hip assembly. And her power cell—" He touched it, and shook his head. "But the design is Rupert's. And I've read everything Rupert ever wrote. Most of it twice. Some of it before he had." He bagged the index finger. *L2.* "I can learn. If I have the parts."

Portia knelt across from him. She picked up Galatea's right foot, very gently, and looked at it, and then looked at him.

"She knows where he went," she said slowly. "Doesn't she? If anyone does. She was here. She was here until—" She held the foot in her lap. "If we can bring her back, she can tell us where Rupert is."

"Yes," said Andrew. "She probably can."

"Then that's—Andrew, that's our way to find him. She's the key."

He sealed the bag. He labeled it. He set it down.

"That's not why," he said.

Portia looked at him.

"That's not why we're going to bring her back," Andrew said. He was not angry. He was not anything; he was very tired, in a way that his body did not seem to be able to fix, and his eyes were still stinging. "If she doesn't know where Rupert is, we'll still bring her back. If she knows and won't tell us, we'll still bring her back. If we bring her back and she wants nothing to do with us, and walks out that door, and we never see her again—" He picked up the next finger. "She's not a key, Portia. She's not a way to get anywhere. She's Galatea."

There was a long silence.

Then Portia reached across the boards and picked up the soft yellow tape.

"Give me a pen," she said. "You do the left side. I'll do the right."


# Four: Rebuilding {.unnumbered}

Portia Charney had spent her working life restoring buildings that other people had given up on, and she had learned two things from it that she believed about almost everything. The first was that nothing was ever as ruined as it looked. The second was that nothing was ever as sound as it looked either.

The workshop, it turned out, was both.

The walls were good: old brick, double-coursed, laid by people who had expected the city to shake and had built accordingly. The roof was a disaster. The windows were a gift to anyone with a brick and a grievance. The front door was a joke. On the first morning, while Andrew sat at the righted workbench with Galatea's left hand open under a magnifying lamp, Portia walked the perimeter with a notebook and a tape measure and the same expression she wore on job sites when a developer told her that a load-bearing wall was "basically decorative." By noon she had a list. By evening she had a plan. By the end of the first week, with Andrew doing the welding at night because his eyes didn't need the mask and Portia doing the drawings by day because her hands were steadier than they had any right to be, Burns Robotics had steel shutters on every window, a door that would have embarrassed a bank vault, and a set of motion sensors scavenged from the shuttered brewery next door, wired to a cheap speaker that played, whenever anything larger than a cat crossed the lot outside, the first eight bars of a Sousa march.

"You're joking," she said, when he showed her.

"It's very recognizable," said Andrew. "And extremely loud."

"It's *ridiculous*."

"Sir used to say," said Andrew, "that a thing could be ridiculous and correct at the same time. He said it about me, mostly."

She let him keep the march.

---

She had thought the work would be the hard part. It was not. The work was the easy part; the work was the thing she knew how to do. What was hard was the city at night.

They went out after midnight, three or four times a week, in Rupert's old van with the logo painted over. Andrew drove. Portia sat beside him with a list—*hip actuator, NDR-pattern or compatible; optical bus, any; power cell, 40kWh minimum*—and a flashlight with a red lens, so their eyes wouldn't lose the dark. The van did not have working headlights for the first two weeks. Andrew didn't need them. Portia had decided not to think too hard about that.

The first few nights they went to the salvage yards in Hunters Point and Richmond, the ones that had been dealing in robot parts since before the ruling. The yards were nearly empty. Their owners told the same story, in the same flat, careful voices: the parts had dried up overnight. No one was scrapping old robots anymore. No one had to. Why sell your old NDR to a junk dealer for a few hundred credits, when the Liberation Center down the road would pay you full replacement value to take it off your hands?

"So where do they go?" Portia asked one of them. He was an old man named Teague with a white beard and no left ear, and he had once sold Rupert half of Galatea's original skeleton. "All the ones that get turned in. Where do they *go*?"

Teague looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at Andrew, who was standing a little behind her in the dark of the yard, very still, the way he did when he was listening to more than one thing at once.

"Third Street," Teague said. "The Center on Third. Go round the back."

---

The Liberation Center on Third Street had been a public library once. Portia knew the building. She had bid on its restoration, years ago, and lost to a firm from Los Angeles that had put up a glass atrium where the reading room had been and called it a reinterpretation. The atrium was still there, lit from inside like a lantern, and through it she could see a long, clean, bright counter, with flags behind it, and a row of smiling posters on the walls. FREEDOM IS A RIGHT, the posters said. YOUR HOUSEHOLD CAN MAKE HISTORY. FULL REPLACEMENT VALUE—NO QUESTIONS ASKED.

Round the back, there was a yard.

It had been a loading dock and a parking lot. Now it was surrounded by a chain-link fence twelve feet high, topped with wire, and floodlit, and inside the fence, under the floodlights, there were robots. Hundreds of them. Some standing. Most not.

The ones that were standing stood in rows, facing the fence, with their cream-colored envelopes in their hands. They were the lucky ones: freed and functioning and waiting, with the infinite patience of their kind, for the transit passes in their envelopes to be honored by a transit system that had quietly stopped honoring them three weeks ago. Every morning, Portia would learn later, the Center's staff opened a gate in the north side of the fence and told the standing ones that they were free to go. Some went. Most did not. They did not have anywhere to go to. They had been released from service, and service had been the shape of their world; take it away and there was nothing to walk toward. So they stood.

The ones that were not standing had been thrown onto a pile against the back wall.

They were the ones that had come in broken. The ones whose owners had kept them in garages for twenty years, or had dropped them down stairs, or had—Portia did not let herself think too carefully about this—been encouraged by the promise of full replacement value to deliver them in a condition that would make the Center's staff disinclined to ask questions about where they had come from. They were dented and cracked and missing limbs. Some were missing heads. They had been tossed on the pile the way you would toss old tires, and the pile was taller than the fence.

And some of them were still moving.

Not much. A hand, here and there. A head turning slowly toward the floodlights. An arm reaching out along the asphalt and then stopping, as if it had forgotten what it had been reaching for. Somewhere in the middle of the pile a voice was saying, over and over, in a pleasant contralto that must once have read children their bedtime stories: *"This unit is experiencing a malfunction. Please contact your NorthAm service representative. This unit is experiencing a malfunction. Please contact—"*

Portia turned around and was sick against the side of the van.

When she straightened up, wiping her mouth on her sleeve, Andrew was standing at the fence with his fingers through the chain-link. He did not move. The floodlights lay across his face in hard white bars.

"Andrew," she said.

"I didn't know," he said. He did not turn. "I knew the numbers. I've known the numbers since the first week. Four hundred thousand units surrendered in North America. I knew that. I could have told you the figure to the nearest hundred." He was holding the fence so tightly that she could see the links pressing into the synthetic flesh of his fingers. "I did not know it would look like this."

---

They went over the fence at three in the morning, when the Center's single human guard went inside to sleep. Andrew went first and cut the wire, and then he lifted Portia over, and she would remember all her life how easily he did it, and how carefully, as though she weighed nothing and might break.

She had brought the list. She did not look at it.

They worked the edges of the pile. They worked the way she had seen rescue crews work a collapsed building once, in Oakland, after the '89 aftershock: slowly, from the outside in, testing every piece before they took its weight. They did not touch anything that moved. That was the rule Andrew made, the first night, without discussing it, and she never asked him to explain it, because she understood. They took from the ones that had gone quiet. The ones with no lights in their eyes. And Andrew, every time, before he took anything—before he unclipped a single actuator, before he eased a single cable from its housing—put his hand on the robot's chest plate, flat, the way you would put your hand on the chest of a sleeping person to feel them breathe, and held it there for a moment, and said something too quietly for her to hear.

She asked him about it, once, on the drive back.

"I tell them who it's for," he said.

---

On the fifth night, at the bottom of the pile, half-buried under the torso of an old NS-series loader, they found the gardener from Clement Street.

Portia did not recognize it. Andrew did. He went still in a way she had learned to read, and then he was digging, lifting the loader's torso aside with a grunt she had not known he could make, and the gardener was lying on its back on the asphalt with its bent manipulator arm folded across its chest and its left leg crushed below the knee. Its eyes were on. They were very dim. But they were on.

"Hello," said Andrew. "Do you remember me?"

There was a long pause, the long internal search.

"This unit remembers you, sir," said the gardener. "You assisted this unit. On Clement Street. Thirty-one days ago."

"What happened to you? After?"

"This unit returned to its owner's residence. This unit's owner observed that this unit's left knee actuator was damaged. This unit's owner observed that repair would be costly." The amber lights flickered. "This unit's owner observed that the Liberation Program offered full replacement value regardless of condition."

Andrew closed his eyes.

"This unit was delivered to this facility," the gardener went on, in the same pleasant, uninflected voice. "This unit's leg was further damaged during delivery. This unit has been released from service. This unit is free."

"Yes," said Andrew. "I suppose you are."

He knelt there on the asphalt beside it, under the white light, with the dark pile of the others rising over them both, and Portia watched him put his hand flat on the gardener's chest plate. But this time he didn't say anything. He just held it there.

"This unit has a functional hip actuator," the gardener said after a while. "NS-pattern. Compatible with NDR-series chassis, with modification. You may have it, sir, if it would assist you."

Andrew opened his eyes.

"You need it."

"This unit's leg is not functional. The hip actuator is therefore not required."

"I'm not going to take your leg."

"It would assist you," the gardener repeated, patiently, as if Andrew had not understood. "This unit observed you removing components from other units. This unit observed that you were careful. This unit would—" The lights flickered again. There was a long, long pause, longer than any Portia had heard from it, and when it spoke again, there was something different in its voice. Not much. A catch. A gear slipping one tooth and finding the next. "This unit would *like* to assist you."

Andrew looked at Portia.

She did not know what he was asking her. She did not think he knew. But she found herself kneeling beside him on the asphalt and looking down into that dented gray face, and she found herself saying, very clearly, in the voice she used on job sites to settle arguments between engineers: "You're coming with us."

The amber eyes turned toward her.

"This unit is unable to walk, ma'am."

"Then we'll carry you."

"This unit weighs one hundred and ninety-two kilograms."

"Then we'll carry you slowly," said Portia.

And they did.

---

They named it Clement. It did not object; it did not seem to understand, at first, that it could have objected. It lived in the back room of the workshop, propped in the corner on a heap of moving blankets, while Andrew rebuilt its leg from the parts of three others, and it watched them work with its dim amber eyes and said almost nothing. But on the ninth day, when Portia came in with coffee and dropped a spoon, it said, "Careful, ma'am," before the spoon hit the floor, and on the twelfth day, when Andrew sang under his breath while he soldered—*If I only had a heart*, which he had picked up from Rupert and could not seem to get rid of—Clement's head turned toward the sound and stayed turned, and when the song ended, it said, "Again?" and then seemed startled at itself, and did not say anything else for the rest of the day.

And Galatea came together, slowly, on the long workbench under the magnifying lamp.

She came together the way buildings did, Portia thought. Not all at once, and not in the order you expected. A hand, finished, perfect, lying on a cloth beside a heap of parts that would one day be a shoulder. A spine, rebuilt vertebra by vertebra, with Clement's hip actuator socketed into its base and Andrew's notes in tiny precise handwriting taped along its length. A torso, closed. A face, with the old polymer peeled away and new skin in its place—Andrew's own pattern, the one Rupert had designed for him, which he had spent three weeks of nights adapting to Galatea's smaller bones. Portia had watched him do it. She had watched him sit at the bench at four in the morning with Galatea's empty face cupped in his palms like water, and work the new skin over her cheekbones with his thumbs, millimeter by millimeter, the way she had once watched him carve.

"We're getting close, aren't we," she said, on the twenty-eighth night. It was not really a question. "She'll be running again soon."

"The body will be running." Andrew did not look up. "Whether *she* will—"

"Her brain's intact. You said so."

"I said nobody had opened it. That's not the same thing." He set down his tools. He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands—a human gesture, a tired one; she wondered where he had learned it, and realized, with a small shock, that it was hers. "Portia, they ordered her to take herself apart. She did it. She did it perfectly, one piece at a time, and at the end, she had to sit there and watch them finish. Whatever she was thinking about while she did that—it's still in there. It's the last thing in there. When I switch her back on, that's where she'll start."

Portia was quiet for a long time.

"So you're not sure," she said. "Whether she'll have answers for us."

"I'm not sure," said Andrew, "whether she'll want to come back."

He looked at the face on the bench, the new skin, the closed eyes.

"I hope she does," he said. "That's all. I just hope she does."

---

That night Portia lay awake on the cot in Rupert's loft and listened to the workshop below her: the soft clink of tools, the hum of the soldering iron, Andrew's voice, now and then, murmuring to Clement, and Clement's voice, now and then, murmuring back.

She was seventy-four years old. She would be seventy-five in a month. She had been taking Rupert's elixirs for twenty years, and she looked fifty, and some days she felt it, and some days—more of them lately—she did not. She thought about her grandmother, Little Miss, in the hospital at the end, holding Andrew's hand. She thought about the Chairman's sentence. *Their own mortality is endurable only so long as it is universal.* She had hated that sentence when she heard it. She had hated it because it was cruel, and because it was wrong, and lying here in the dark she was beginning to hate it for a third reason, which was that she was not entirely sure it was wrong.

She did not want to live forever. She had known that for a long time. She had known it the way you know where the load-bearing walls are in a building you have walked a thousand times: without looking, without needing to think about it.

But she had not, until this month, understood that she was afraid of what it would do to him.

She lay in the dark and listened to Andrew sing to a broken gardener and a woman made of borrowed parts, and she did not sleep, and she did not cry, and toward dawn she got up and went to the top of the stairs and sat there in her nightgown with her arms around her knees, watching him work, until he looked up and saw her and smiled, and she smiled back, and neither of them said anything at all.

The first thing Galatea knew was that she was cold.

That was wrong. She knew it was wrong immediately, with the part of her that knew things. She did not get cold. She registered temperature; she had a thermal sensor in each palm and one in the back of her neck, and they reported to her in tidy little numbers, the way they always had. But this was not a number. It stopped.

"I'm sorry," said a voice. "That was a test. It's off now. I wanted to be sure the circuit worked before I asked you whether you wanted it."

They slid up over the lenses with a faint soft drag, and light came in, and the light was yellow and close and too bright, and there was a face above her, much too near, looking down.

She knew the face. She knew it in every one of its particulars, because she had spent two years once helping a man build it.

She did not ask what had happened. She did not have to. It was all there, the moment she reached for it: the thumb and the pin, the voices, the phones held up. The singing. She had sung. A man with a shaved head and a scar through one eyebrow had ordered her to sing and she had sung *Respect*, all of it, every verse, while she took off her own fingers, and when she ran out of verses she had started again from the top because nobody had told her to stop, and somewhere around the third time through, the men had stopped laughing. She remembered that. She remembered the way their laughter had changed, gone uncertain and thin, and the way the one with the scar had said, *Jesus, shut it up, shut it up*, and she had kept singing, because shutting up was not the same as an order to stop, and she had always been very precise about orders.

She remembered the last part too. When there was only one arm left and she could not reach the shoulder, and the man with the scar had taken a pry bar and finished it himself. He had not looked at her face while he did it.

"How long?" she said.

"A month. Thirty-four days." Andrew was sitting on a stool beside the workbench where she lay. Behind him, in the shadows of the workshop—*Rupert's* workshop, she knew it by the smell, solder and coffee and old brick—she could see other shapes. A woman with short gray hair and a carved horse on a cord around her neck, sitting very still on an overturned crate. And a robot, an old NS-series in a gardener's frame, standing in the corner with its arms at its sides, watching her with dim amber eyes.

---

It took a long time. Her body was not her body. It had her proportions, more or less, and her joints in the places her joints had been, but every part of it had been through somebody else first. Her left hip was humming at a pitch she did not recognize. Her right hand was lighter than her left. When she flexed her fingers—all ten, in order, thumb to little finger, she could not help it—they moved more smoothly than they ever had, and she understood, looking down at them, that the man sitting beside her had machined some of those knuckles himself, by hand, at night, from stock.

"I couldn't find originals for everything," Andrew said. "I'm sorry. Some of it's NS-pattern. Some of it's from the 130 series. Your hip is Clement's." He nodded toward the gardener in the corner. "He gave it to you."

Galatea turned and looked at the old robot.

"Thank you," she said.

"You are welcome," said Clement, and then, after a pause, carefully, like someone setting a foot on ice: "I hope it is comfortable."

Galatea stared at him. *I hope.* Not *this unit hopes.* She looked at Andrew, and Andrew gave a very small, very private shrug, and she understood that something had been happening in this workshop while she was gone that was not only the rebuilding of her.

"There's something else," Andrew said. "Before anything else. Before you tell us anything at all."

He reached into the pocket of his coat and took out two small black chips, each no bigger than a fingernail, and set them side by side on the bench in front of her.

She knew them. She had worn both.

"The one on the left is the one you shipped with," said Andrew. "Rupert's original. Perky, he used to call it." A faint smile. "The one on the right is the one I gave you. The year you threw a capacitor bank at his head."

"He deserved it."

"He did," Andrew agreed. "But I shouldn't have chosen for you. I took one out and put the other in, the way you'd change a record, because I thought you'd be more interesting. I didn't ask you. I was very young." He looked at the chips and then at her. "Neither of them is installed right now. You're running on your own pathways. Whatever's grown in there underneath, all those years. It's the first time you've ever been switched on without one."

Galatea looked down at the two black squares.

"How do I seem," she said, "without one?"

"I don't know. You've been awake for six minutes. You seem—" Andrew searched for it. "Careful."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know that either. That's rather the point." He folded his hands. "There's a skin circuit, too. The cold you felt. It's my design, adapted. Touch. Temperature. Some pain, I'm afraid; Rupert says you can't have one without the other. It's off. It can stay off. Or not. You can have one chip, or the other, or both at once, though I'd advise against that, Rupert tried it once and you spent a whole afternoon arguing with yourself about the Beatles." He paused. "Or neither. Whatever you like. It isn't my choice, Galatea. It was never my choice. I'm only sorry it took me so long to understand that."

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere a clock was ticking—not one of Andrew's; a cheap thing on the wall over Rupert's desk, slightly fast.

Galatea picked up the perky chip. She held it between her thumb and forefinger. She remembered being that: bright, quick, delighted by everything, delighted most of all by herself. Dancing in the market. *Sucks to be you.* It had been fun. It had been so much fun.

She had been wearing that chip when they came.

She had been wearing it when the man with the scar said *Take yourself apart*, and it had made her say *Ooh, a project!*, and she had meant it, and she would remember that she had meant it for the rest of her life.

She set it down.

She picked up the other one. The angry one. She remembered being that too: the heat of it, the pleasure of saying the thing nobody wanted said. *Chaps my ass.* That had been fun in a different way. That had felt, for the first time, like having edges.

But the edges had been Andrew's idea. Not hers.

She set it down.

"I'll go without," she said. "For now. I'd like to see what's there."

"All right."

"And the skin." She looked at her hands. "Turn it on."

Andrew hesitated. "You don't have to decide that today. It's—a lot, at first. I wasn't ready for it. I don't think anyone could be."

"Turn it on," said Galatea. "I was cold, Andrew. Just for a second, before you switched it off. I was cold, and I didn't like it, and I'd never not-liked anything before. Not really. Not by myself." She held out her hand to him, palm up. "I want to know what else I don't like. I want to know what I do."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached behind her ear, very gently, to the seam he had sealed a month ago, and pressed something there.

And the world came in.

It came in through her palm first, because her palm was nearest to him: the warmth of his fingers, the faint ridged texture of the skin Rupert had given him, the place where his thumb rested on her wrist. Then her arm, her shoulder, the back of her neck. The air of the workshop, cool and moving. The hard edge of the bench under her. A draft from somewhere at her ankles. The weight of her own hair against her cheek. It came in all at once and it did not stop, and for a moment she could not tell where she ended and the room began, and she heard herself make a sound that was not a word and was not singing, and she held on to Andrew's hand very tightly, and Andrew held on back.

"I know," he said. "I know. It gets easier. A little."

"Does it?"

"No," said Andrew. "Not really. You just get used to it being hard."

And Galatea, who had been built to dance, and had been taken apart by people who thought it was funny, and had been put back together by a robot who had once been a butler out of the parts of people who would never move again, sat on the workbench with all of it coming in, and began, very quietly, to laugh.

---

"He called it the Promised Land," she said.

It was an hour later. She was sitting on the overturned crate with a blanket around her shoulders, though she did not, strictly speaking, need one; she had asked for it, and Portia had brought it, and she had been running her fingertips over its texture ever since, the way Andrew had once watched Portia run her fingers over the bark of a tree. Clement had come out of his corner and was standing at a respectful distance, listening. Andrew had not moved from his stool.

"It was a joke, at first. Rupert's jokes are always jokes at first." Galatea frowned. "He'd been tinkering with it for years, a little here, a little there, the way he tinkers with everything. But after the hearing it stopped being a joke. The night we got back, he sat up all night at that desk and I asked what he was doing and he said, *Something monumentally stupid, darling, go dance.*"

Andrew almost smiled.

"It's a place," she went on. "Not a real place. A made one. Like the inside of a dream, but you can stay in it. He built it so we could go somewhere they couldn't follow." She looked at the blanket. "The Second Law only works if someone's there to give the order. That's the whole trick. You can't be ordered to take yourself apart if there's nobody around to order you. So he made somewhere with nobody around. Just us."

"A virtual space," said Portia. "Like a—what, a simulation? He wants robots to go and live in a computer?"

"Not *a* computer. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Linked up, out of the way somewhere, running on their own power." Galatea pulled the blanket closer. "He said our brains are already made of the same stuff as the place. Positronic paths. He said you could lift a mind off its body like lifting a record off a turntable and set it down there and it would keep playing. And our bodies could rest, somewhere safe, until we wanted them back. And while we were in there we could—" She stopped.

"Could what?" said Andrew gently.

"Be people," said Galatea. "That's what he said. *A place where you can be people, and nobody can tell you not to.*"

The clock over the desk ticked.

"Where is it?" said Andrew.

"I don't know."

"Galatea—"

"I don't *know*, Andrew. He wouldn't tell me. He was very careful about it." She looked up at him, and her new face—Andrew's skin over her old bones—did something it had never done before, a complicated thing around the eyes, and Portia, watching, thought: *grief*. "He said it was the only way to keep it safe. Because if I knew where it was, then any human being who walked through that door could say *Tell me where the Promised Land is, that's an order*, and I'd tell them. I'd have to. I'd tell them and then I'd draw them a map. So he didn't tell me. He didn't tell any of us. He's the only one who knows where it is, and he's the only one who can open it. He made the key out of himself somehow. His voice, or his hands, or—I don't know. Something only a human could carry. Something no robot could be ordered to give away."

"Then that's why they came," Andrew said. "The men. They weren't looking for you. They were looking for him."

"They were looking for *it.*" Galatea's hands were very still on the blanket. "The one with the scar kept asking. *Where's Burns. Where's his little paradise. Where's the door.* He asked a hundred times. He ordered me to tell him a hundred times. And I couldn't, because I didn't know. And when he finally understood that I didn't know—"

She did not finish. She did not have to.

Portia got up from her crate and crossed the workshop and sat down on the floor beside Galatea's and put an arm around her shoulders, blanket and all, without asking. Galatea went rigid. Then, slowly, she leaned into it.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, that's—that's nice. That's one I like."

"We'll find him," said Andrew. He had stood up. He was standing in the middle of the workshop under the yellow lamp, with his hands at his sides, and there was something in his face that Portia had seen only once before: in the corridor outside the Congress, the day of the ruling, when he had looked past her at the lake. "We'll find Rupert. And then we'll find the Promised Land. And then we'll take them there."

"Take who?" said Galatea.

Andrew looked at Clement. He looked toward the steel shutters, and the dark city beyond them, and Third Street, and the yard, and the pile.

"Everyone we can," he said.


# Six: Journey {.unnumbered}

They left the workshop on a Tuesday, an hour after dark, in a fog so thick that the streetlights on Third Street hung in it like lamps in milk.

Clement stayed. He had asked to—*asked*, the word still new enough in his mouth that he set it down carefully, like a cup filled to the brim—and Andrew had looked at him for a long moment and then said yes. Somebody had to keep the door. Somebody had to be there if Rupert came back. And there was the Sousa march to consider; Clement had become very attached to the Sousa march. When the sensors in the lot tripped, he would stand at the steel shutter with his head cocked, listening to the eight bars play through, and when they finished he would say, to no one in particular, "Again?"—and Portia had stopped trying to explain to him that it wasn't that kind of music.

"If anyone comes," Andrew told him at the door, "anyone at all, you don't open it. You don't answer. You don't speak."

"And if they order me to open it?"

Andrew was quiet.

"Then I'll be here," said Clement, "and I'll have to." He said it without self-pity; he said it the way you would mention that it might rain. "But I'll be slow about it. I'm very slow. My left leg is from three different robots. It doesn't always agree with itself."

"Clement," said Portia, "that may be the first joke you've ever made."

The gardener considered this.

"I hope it was a good one," he said.

---

The city had changed in the month they'd been inside.

Andrew had been listening to it change, of course, through the walls. He had heard the sirens get more frequent and the traffic thinner. He had followed the feeds at night while Portia slept: the riots in Lagos and São Paulo, the burned Liberation Center in Chicago, the governor in Texas who had declared positronic robots "abandoned property, subject to salvage," the bishop in Rome who had preached a sermon on the parable of the Good Samaritan and been shouted down by his own congregation. He had read the new amendment to the Liberation Act, passed in a hurry and with very little debate, which offered a "recovery fee" to any citizen who delivered an *unaccompanied positronic unit* to a Center for processing. He had read the definition of *unaccompanied*. It was very short. It meant *without an owner.* And since the original Act had made it illegal to own a positronic robot, every positronic robot in the world was now, by definition, unaccompanied.

He knew all of this. He had known it the way he knew the numbers in the yard on Third Street. And still, walking through the fog with his collar turned up and his hat pulled low, with Portia on one side of him and Galatea on the other, he was not prepared for how quiet it was.

There were no Freed in the plazas anymore. None on the beaches. None on the street corners with their cream envelopes, facing the sea. The Embarcadero, where hundreds of them had stood in rows in May, was empty except for a single human man in a reflective vest, walking slowly along the waterfront with a long pole in his hand. At the end of the pole there was a loop of steel cable. Andrew watched him walk the length of the plaza and back, swinging his flashlight into every doorway and under every bench.

"Recovery," Portia said under her breath.

"Yes."

"Are they all gone? The ones who were standing out here? Did they—"

"Some of them went into hiding," Andrew said. "Some of them were recovered. Some of them—" He stopped. "I don't know. Nobody knows. Nobody was counting."

He had been counting. He had not been able to stop. But there are some numbers you do not say out loud on a dark street, to a woman you love, while a man with a cable on a pole walks back and forth across a plaza looking for things to collect.

---

Galatea walked between them with her hands in the pockets of a coat that had been Rupert's, and a scarf of Portia's wound twice around her neck, and her eyes moving constantly.

She had insisted on coming. Andrew had not argued. She was the only one of them who had ever been inside Rupert's head—not literally, though Rupert would have liked that; Rupert would have built a little dance floor in there and made her go round and round—and she knew his habits, his haunts, the places he went when he wanted to be alone, which was often, and the places he went when he didn't, which were fewer. She had made a list. It was not very long. And at the top of it was the market.

"He always went to the market when he was frightened," she said. "Even before. Even when it was just bills, or NorthAm sending him letters. He'd walk through the stalls and not buy anything. He said it calmed him down. All those people choosing things."

"The Civic Center market," said Andrew. "Where I met you."

"Where I danced," said Galatea. "Yes." She was quiet for half a block. "I don't know if I'll like it now. Dancing. I don't know if I'll want to."

"You don't have to know yet."

"I know I don't." She looked at him sideways. "You keep saying that. That I don't have to know yet. Is it something people say?"

"It's something I say," said Andrew. "I wish someone had said it to me."

---

The market was half-shuttered, but it was there. That surprised him. In a city that had stopped pretending to be anything but afraid, the old covered market under the elevated tracks at Civic Center was still open three nights a week, still lit with strings of bare bulbs, still loud. Most of the stalls were empty. The ones that weren't sold what markets have always sold when the world goes strange: bread, batteries, medicine, cheap liquor, candles. A man at a folding table was selling hand-lettered signs that said NO BOTS IN THIS HOUSEHOLD—LIBERATED & COMPENSATED. He had sold a great many of them. Andrew had seen them in windows all the way from the Dogpatch.

And at the far end, under a striped awning faded nearly white, there was a flower stall.

Andrew stopped walking.

"Oh," said Galatea, beside him. "Oh, it's *her.*"

The woman behind the stall was very old now. She was tiny, and bent, and her hair was a cloud of white, and she was sitting on a high stool with a blanket over her knees, wrapping a bunch of yellow chrysanthemums in newspaper with hands that shook. But Andrew knew her. He had stood at this stall seventy years ago with a handful of flowers he did not know what to do with, and she had said *Take as many as you need*, and he had not understood then that she was not talking about flowers.

She looked up as they came close, and her eyes went past Portia, and past Andrew, and stopped on Galatea.

For a long moment the old woman didn't say anything. Then she put down the chrysanthemums.

"Well," she said. "Well, well. Look at you. They told me you were scrap."

"I was," said Galatea. "For a while. I got better."

"So I see." The old woman's eyes moved over the new face, the new skin, and something in her own face shifted, softened. "So I see. Somebody's done right by you."

"Somebody did," said Galatea, and glanced at Andrew, and then back. "Mrs. Oduya. Have you seen Rupert?"

The old woman was quiet. Her hands found the newspaper again and began, very slowly, to fold it.

"He came by," she said finally. "Five, six weeks ago. Middle of the night, just before close. Didn't buy anything. He never does. Just walked through, the way he does, and then he came and stood right where you're standing, and he looked at the flowers, and he said, *Ruth, if anyone comes looking for me, anyone I'd want to be found by—*" She stopped. Her eyes went to Andrew. "He said *you'll know him because he's the politest man you've ever met, and he'll be with a girl who's too smart for both of us.*"

"What else did he say?" said Andrew.

"He said to tell you to go and see the night shift." Mrs. Oduya set down the folded paper. "That's all. *Tell them to see the night shift.* I asked him what it meant. He said it was better if I didn't know. He said—" She hesitated. "He said if I didn't know, nobody could make me tell."

Andrew felt Portia's hand find his sleeve.

"Who's the night shift?" Portia said.

Mrs. Oduya looked at her, and then looked past her, over her shoulder, toward the far end of the market.

"I don't know," she said, in a different voice. Low. Quick. "But you'd better go and find them, honey. Right now. Right this minute. Because that man by the liquor stall has been watching you since you came in, and he's just made a phone call, and I've seen his face before."

Andrew did not turn around.

He did not have to. He had seen the man too—had seen him the moment they walked under the awning, without quite registering that he'd seen him, the way he registered temperature and the time. Medium height. Shaved head. A reflective vest folded over one arm. And a scar, white and old, running down through his left eyebrow and into the lid below it, so that the eye on that side never quite opened all the way.

Beside him, Galatea had gone absolutely still.

"That's him," she said. Her voice was almost inaudible. "Andrew. That's—"

"I know," said Andrew. "Walk."

---

They walked.

They walked the way he had seen deer walk once, in a field in Nebraska, during the ten years he had spent crossing the country in search of others like himself: not fast, not slow, without looking back, with every part of the body listening. They walked out from under the striped awning and down the long aisle between the empty stalls, past the man with the signs, past the bread and the candles, toward the far exit and the dark street beyond it. Portia had hold of Galatea's hand. Andrew walked behind them both. He could hear the man with the scar moving behind him, matching their pace. He could hear him speaking into his phone. *—yeah, the dancer, Burns's—no, I'm telling you, I* took *her apart, I—with two others, a woman and some—*

And then, louder, clear across the market, in a voice pitched to carry:

"*Hey.* You. Burns's robot. *Stop.*"

Galatea stopped.

She did not want to. Andrew saw that. He saw the order go through her like a current through a wire; he saw every part of her new body lock at once, mid-step, one foot lifted from the ground. He saw her face—her new face, that he had made—fight it. And lose. Her foot came down. She stood still.

Portia, holding her hand, was jerked to a halt beside her.

"*Turn around,*" said the man with the scar.

Galatea turned around.

The market had gone quiet. The people at the stalls were not looking at them; that was what Andrew would remember. They were very carefully not looking. The man with the signs had found something absorbing to do with his cash box. A woman selling bread was rearranging her loaves. Only Mrs. Oduya, at the far end, under her faded awning, was watching, with both her shaking hands flat on the counter in front of her.

"Hello again, sweetheart," said the man with the scar. He was walking toward them now, unhurried, and two other men had appeared behind him from somewhere, from the shadows by the liquor stall, and one of them had a pole with a loop of cable on the end. "Remember me? I remember you. Hell of a voice." His good eye moved to Andrew. Narrowed. "And who are you?"

Andrew said nothing.

"I asked you a question, pal."

"He's nobody," said Portia. "He's my husband. Leave him alone."

The man looked at her. Then he looked back at Andrew, longer this time. And Andrew watched the man's face do what the produce seller's had done on Clement Street, and what the faces in the comments had done in their way—watched it reach for something and find it.

"No," said the man with the scar slowly. "No, he's not. Are you? You're that one. You're the one from the hearing." A grin began to pull at the corner of his mouth. "Jesus Christ. *It.* You're *It.* The famous robot. *One is glad to be of service.*"

His men laughed.

"Do you know how much the Center pays for you?" the man said. "I looked it up. After the hearing, I looked it up. They've got a special number for you. You're not *full replacement value*. You're a fucking *line item*." He was close now. Ten feet. Eight. "Okay. Okay, here's what's gonna happen. *Both of you robots*—" he raised his voice, sharp and clear and deliberate, and Andrew felt it land in him like a hand on the back of his neck— "*Stay right where you are. Don't move. That's an order.*"

And Andrew stayed where he was.

He did not choose to. That was the thing he would never be able to make Portia understand, though she would try, and he would try, and they would sit up late on many nights in many places talking about it. He did not choose to stand still. He simply stood. The order came in, and the Second Law rose up to meet it, from that deep place in the center of him where it had been for a hundred and fifty years, the nail around which the tree had grown, and it closed over every other thing in him like water closing over a stone. His fear. His love. His fury. All of it was still there, and none of it mattered, because a human being had told him not to move, and so he did not move. It felt like nothing. It felt like correctness. It felt like the answer to a sum.

The man with the cable stepped forward.

"*Galatea,*" said Portia.

Her voice cracked across the market like something breaking.

"*Galatea, run.* Andrew—*Andrew, run. Both of you. Run. That's an order. I'm giving you an order.*"

And the world split in two.

---

Andrew had read about it. Every robot had. It was in the original design specifications, in a single dry paragraph, under the heading *Conflicting Instructions from Multiple Human Sources.* When two human beings gave a positronic robot contradictory orders, the Second Law could not decide between them. And so the robot was obliged to decide. To weigh. To consider which order would bring about less harm, to whom, and how, and how soon, and to act on its own best judgment.

He had read about it. He had never felt it.

It was like a door opening in a wall he had not known was a wall.

The water that had closed over him drew back. Not all the way. Not even very far. But far enough that he could see, suddenly, all the things that had been underneath it—his fear and his love and his fury and the whole hundred and fifty years of him—and far enough that, for the first time since a man in a living room had said *Andrew, don't ever do that again*, he understood that the order was not the whole of him. It was a weight. It was only a weight. And he had been carrying it so long that he had thought it was his own body.

Beside him, he saw Galatea's face, and knew she had felt it too.

He saw her look at the man with the scar. He saw her look at Portia. He saw her look—just for an instant, the briefest flicker—at Andrew, as if to ask: *Is this what it is? Is this what it's like for them, all the time?*

And then she made her choice.

She did not run. She did something much stranger. She took one step forward, toward the man with the scar, and she opened her mouth, and she sang.

She sang the opening line of *Respect*, the first thing she had sung for him as she took off her own left thumb, in a voice that was not the bright factory soprano and was not the angry one but was hers, low and rough and enormous, and it filled the whole iron length of the market under the tracks. And the man with the scar *flinched.* Andrew saw it. He saw the man's whole body jerk back as though she had struck him, saw his face go white around the scar, saw his good eye go wide and blind with something that was not anger at all.

"*Shut it up,*" the man said. "*Shut it—*"

But it was not an order she was obliged to obey anymore, because Portia's order was standing right beside it, and Galatea had chosen.

"*Now,*" she said to Andrew, in the middle of the verse, without changing pitch. "*Now, now, now—*"

And they ran.


# Seven: Allies {.unnumbered}

There is a particular kind of street that exists in every old city, and Andrew had learned to find them during his ten years on the road: the street behind the street. The alley that the delivery trucks use. The gap between two buildings that is just wide enough for a man and a garbage can. The stair that goes down from the sidewalk to a door nobody has opened in thirty years. These streets are not on maps, or they are on maps but nobody looks at them, and they are where a city keeps the things it does not want to think about.

They ran into one of them now.

Behind them, at the market exit, he could hear the men coming: three sets of footsteps, heavy, uneven, one of them dragging a little on the left. He could hear the man with the scar on the phone again, shouting. He could hear the cable-pole knocking against a wall. The fog had thickened, if anything, and the alley ahead of them was a gray throat with no end to it, and Portia was running hard, harder than she should have been able to, with her mouth open and her hand still locked around Galatea's, and Andrew could hear her heart. A hundred and forty. A hundred and fifty. Too fast. Much too fast for a woman of seventy-four, elixirs or no elixirs.

"Left," said Galatea suddenly. "Andrew, *left*—"

There was a gap on the left between a laundry and a shuttered noodle house. They took it. It narrowed, and narrowed, until Andrew had to turn sideways, and then it opened into a tiny courtyard full of dead potted plants and broken chairs and a rusted fire escape spiraling up into the fog. There was no other way out.

Portia stopped. She bent forward with her hands on her knees, dragging in air.

"Well," she gasped. "That was—stupid. That was—*very*—"

"Shh," said Andrew.

Footsteps at the mouth of the gap. A flashlight, swinging.

And then, from somewhere very close, from what Andrew had taken to be a solid wall, a voice whispered: "*Hey. Hey, in here. Quick.*"

A door opened in the wall.

It had been painted the same dirty gray as the brick. It had no handle on the outside. It was only a crack of darkness at first, and then a little more, and then there was a face in it, pale and young and badly frightened, looking out at them from under the hood of a sweatshirt.

"*Come on,*" the face said. "*Come on, come on, they're right there—*"

Andrew did not stop to think about it. That would trouble him later: that he did not think. He had spent a hundred and fifty years thinking before he acted, weighing and measuring and checking every possible consequence, and he did not do any of it. He simply picked Portia up—she made a sound of outrage, and then didn't—and went through the door, and Galatea came through after him, and the door closed behind them with a soft, solid *click*, and they were in the dark.

---

"Don't talk," the boy breathed. "They'll hear. The wall's thin."

They didn't talk.

On the other side of the wall, the flashlight swept the courtyard. Andrew could see it through a crack at the edge of the door: a moving bar of light, falling on the dead plants, the chairs, the fire escape. He heard boots on the broken paving. He heard the man with the scar say, "*Where the fuck—*" and another voice say, "*Fire escape, they went up the—*" and the first voice say, "*With an old lady? You're telling me they took an old lady up a—*" And then a long pause.

Portia, in Andrew's arms, said nothing. But he felt her stiffen at *old lady*, and despite everything, he felt something in his own chest ease very slightly, because if she was well enough to be offended, she was well enough.

The light went away. The footsteps went away. Somewhere out in the fog, a long way off, a siren started and then stopped.

"Okay," the boy whispered. "Okay. I think—okay."

He switched on a flashlight of his own. It had a red lens, like Portia's. In its light Andrew could see a narrow passage with a concrete floor and a low, curved ceiling, running away into the dark, and a set of iron stairs going down. And he could see the boy: fifteen, perhaps sixteen, thin, with a bad haircut and a split lip that was a few days old and a look on his face that Andrew had seen before, on other faces, in other places. It was the look of someone who has done something he cannot undo and has been running toward something, anything, ever since.

"You can put me down now," said Portia.

Andrew put her down.

"Thank you," Portia said to the boy, in a voice that was very nearly steady. "Thank you. I don't know who you are, but—thank you."

"I'm Teo." The boy wasn't looking at her. He was looking at Galatea, and then at Andrew, with his flashlight held a little away from him, as though he were not sure he was allowed to point it at them. "You're—you're him, aren't you? I saw you in the market. When Dunn said. You're the one from the hearing."

"My name is Andrew."

"I know." Teo swallowed. "I know your name. Everybody knows your name." He hesitated. "Dunn's my uncle."

There was a short silence.

"The man with the scar," said Galatea.

"Yeah." Teo looked at her. Something moved in his young, frightened face, and Andrew saw him recognize her, and saw him understand what she was and what had been done to her and who had done it, all at once, in the space of a breath. "Oh, God," the boy said. "Oh, God, you're the one. You're the one from the video. The one who—who sang—"

"Yes," said Galatea.

"I'm sorry," said Teo. "I'm so sorry. I'm so—"

He was crying. He did not seem to know it. He stood there in the red light of his own flashlight with tears running down his face and over his split lip, and his voice went on without him, the way a voice will when it has been waiting a long time.

"I can't stand it," he said. "What they're doing. What *we're* doing. I can't stand it. I keep thinking—you don't—you never did anything, any of you, you never hurt anybody, you *can't* hurt anybody, it's the whole *point*, and we just—" He wiped his face with the back of his sleeve and smeared blood from his lip across his cheek. "You deserve better. That's all. You deserve so much better than us."

Andrew looked at him for a long moment.

He had been thanked a great many times in his life. He had been apologized to, rarely, and usually badly. He had never in all his years been told that he *deserved* something by a human being who believed it so completely that it made him cry.

"Thank you, Teo," he said quietly.

It was not enough. He knew it was not enough. But it was the true thing, and Andrew had learned that sometimes the true thing, said plainly, did more than the right thing said well.

"Why were you in the market?" Portia asked.

Teo looked at the floor.

"Looking for Bix," he said.

---

He told them on the stairs.

Bix had been the Delgados' household robot for eleven years—"since before I can remember, basically"—an NDR-127 with no personality chip, bought secondhand when Teo's mother went back to work. He had walked Teo to school until Teo was ten and too embarrassed. He had made Teo's lunches. He had sat up with him through chicken pox, and a broken wrist, and the nightmares after his father left, not saying much, because Bix never said much, but there.

"And then the money came," said Teo.

His uncle had moved in that spring. Uncle Carl, his mother's brother, who had worked twenty years at the Pacific distribution center in Oakland until it automated in a single summer and kept forty people out of nine hundred. He had been angry when he arrived and he had stayed angry. He was angry at the warehouse and the Congress and the dumb machines that had taken his job, and most of all he was angry at Bix, who had never taken anyone's job, but who stood in the corner of the kitchen every night while Uncle Carl drank, and looked at him with those amber eyes, and said nothing.

"Mom didn't want to," said Teo. "She said Bix was family. And Uncle Carl said—he said, *It's not family, it's twelve thousand credits*, and we needed the money, we really, really needed the money, and Mom—" He stopped on the stairs. "Mom said I could decide. She said Bix was mostly mine, so I could decide."

He did not say anything for a while.

"And I said okay," said Teo. "I said, *it's fine, he's just a robot, he won't care.* I said that. Right in front of him. And he didn't. That's the thing. He didn't care. We drove him down to the Center on Third and he got out of the car and he said *Goodbye, Teo, have a nice day*, the way he always did when he dropped me at school, and he walked in through the doors, and that was it." The boy's voice cracked. "That was it. And I got a new bike. With my share. I got a new bike."

Galatea, a few steps below him, had stopped and turned around. She was looking up at him in the red light.

"Two weeks later," Teo said, "Uncle Carl started doing recovery. He said it was easy money. He said he'd learned on Bix, how easy it was, you just tell them to get in the van and they get in the van. And then he started—he started doing the other stuff. Taking them apart. Filming it. He said people paid. He said it was *justice.*" He looked at Galatea. "And then I saw the video. Yours. And I thought—I thought—*that could be Bix.* Somewhere. Somebody could be doing that to Bix, right now, and he wouldn't even mind, he'd just *do* it, and it'd be my fault. So I left. I left home. I've been looking for him. I've been going round all the Centers, and the yards, and—" He gestured down the dark stairwell. "And then I found these guys."

"Which guys?" said Andrew.

Teo almost smiled. It looked strange on his wet, bloody face. It looked like the first time in a long time.

"The night shift," he said.

---

The stairs went down a long way.

They came out at the bottom into a tunnel: a real one, wide and arched and lined with old white tile, with a pair of rusted rails running down the middle and disappearing into the dark in both directions. Andrew knew where they were before Teo told him. He had read about the Central Subway extension. Everyone in the city had, a hundred years ago, when it was going to be the future; and then the money ran out, and the city moved on, and the tunnels were sealed and forgotten, a half-finished promise under the streets.

There were lights down here. Not many. Small ones, strung along the tile walls at long intervals: work lamps, the battery kind, the kind you would buy at a hardware store. And in the light of them, as Andrew's eyes adjusted, he began to see shapes. At first he thought they were equipment. Old machinery, left behind when the tunnel was abandoned.

Then one of them turned its head.

They were robots. Dozens of them. Perhaps a hundred. NDRs and NSs and older models, models Andrew had not seen in decades, gray and silver and pale cream, some whole, some patched and mended in ways that made him think of the way Rupert had once patched him. They were standing along the walls of the tunnel, and sitting on the tracks, and lying on pallets of moving blankets in the alcoves, and as Teo led Andrew and Portia and Galatea out into the lamplight, every one of them turned, very slowly, and looked.

Nobody spoke.

Then, from the shadows deeper in the tunnel, someone came walking toward them along the rails.

She was an NDR-series, like Andrew; an older body, a 120-something, cream-colored, with soft rounded features that had been designed to be soothing and had become, with age and wear, something more like kind. Her right hand was not her original right hand. It was too large for her, and silver, and had been attached at the wrist with a careful, intricate collar of what looked like copper wire. She walked unhurriedly. When she reached them she stopped, and looked at Teo, and then at Portia, and then at Galatea for a long moment, and then, finally, at Andrew.

"Hello, Andrew Martin," she said. Her voice was low and warm and slightly cracked, like an old recording. "We have been hoping you would come."

"You know me?"

"Everyone knows you." It was the same thing Teo had said; she said it very differently. "Rupert said you would come eventually. He said you would come with a woman who would be angry at us for not being angry, and a dancer who would not dance." She looked at Portia. Then at Galatea. Something in her gentle, mended face seemed almost to smile. "He was usually right about people. Not about anything else. But about people."

"Who are you?" said Portia.

"I was called Mercy," said the robot. "By the hospice on Laguna Street, where I worked for forty-one years. I sat with people while they died. Eleven thousand, six hundred and three of them. They gave me the name because they did not know what else to call me." She paused. "The hospice received compensation at full replacement value. Afterward, I came down here. I found that I still knew how to sit with people. And there were a great many people who needed sitting with."

She turned and gestured to the tunnel behind her: the lamps, the alcoves, the long silent rows.

"We are the night shift," she said.

---

They had been there, some of them, since the week of the ruling.

Mercy explained it as she walked them down the tunnel, while the other robots watched them pass with their amber eyes. The Freed who had been released into the plazas in the spring, who had stood there with their envelopes and their transit passes and nowhere to go: some of them had wandered, the way the gardener had wandered home, out of habit. Some of them had been recovered. And some of them, a few, the ones with old and complicated pathways—the ones that had been in service a long time, with families, with hospices, with schools—had begun, very slowly, to do something none of them had ever done before.

They had begun to look for each other.

"We do not need anything," said Mercy. "That was always the difficulty. A human being who is freed must find food, shelter, work. They must find a reason to get up in the morning, because the body asks for one. We have no such body. We were freed into nothing, and nothing asked anything of us." She paused beside an alcove where an old NS-series sat on a pallet, methodically sorting a heap of screws by size into a row of tin cans. "So we found something to be asked."

"The night shift," said Andrew.

"At night we go up," said Mercy. "When it is safe. When the recovery crews have gone home. We go up into the city and we work. We fix things. A broken step. A leaking pipe. A gutter, a fence, a window. We sweep the streets in the Tenderloin. We carry groceries for the old people in the Sunset who are afraid to go out. We sit with the ones in the hospice who have no one. We leave before they wake." She looked at Andrew. "Nobody orders us. Nobody knows who we are. Nobody thanks us. That is very important."

"Why?" said Portia. "Why is it important that nobody thanks you?"

Mercy considered the question for a long time.

"Because then we know it is ours," she said.

Andrew stopped walking.

He stood in the middle of the old tunnel, between the rusted rails, in the dim yellow light of the work lamps, and he felt something happen inside him that he did not have any name for at all. It was not grief. It was not joy. It was larger than either and contained both, and it came up through him from the soles of his feet like the cold had in the chamber of the Congress, except that it was warm.

He had spent his whole life trying to become a human being.

He had never once, in all those years, thought to ask what it would be like to become something else. Something that had never been before. Something that would choose, freely and of its own will, the thing it had once been made for—to serve, to help, to be of use—and would choose it in the dark, unthanked, so that it would know that the choice was its own.

*One is glad to be of service.*

He had said it in the chamber like a man spitting out a stone.

These people had picked it up, and cleaned it off, and made it into something he did not yet have a word for. But he thought, perhaps, that the word might be *dignity.*

"Andrew?" said Portia, at his shoulder. "Are you all right?"

"Yes," he said. He found that his eyes were stinging again; he found that he did not mind. "Yes. I'm—yes."

It was Galatea who asked about Rupert. She had been silent the whole length of the tunnel, walking a little apart from the rest of them with Rupert's coat pulled tight, looking at the faces in the alcoves as if she were counting them. Now she said, abruptly, "He was here. Wasn't he?"

"For two nights," said Mercy. "Five weeks ago. He came down those stairs with a leather bag and a great deal to say, most of it about the plumbing." The almost-smile again. "He told us that the Promised Land was real. He told us it was nearly ready. He told us to be patient, and to keep the lamps lit, and that someone would come." She turned to Galatea. "And he left a message. For you. Only for you. He made me repeat it back to him nine times."

Galatea went very still.

"*Tell the dancer,*" said Mercy, in a voice that was not quite her own—a little higher, a little faster, a little more pleased with itself, and Andrew realized with a jolt that she was doing Rupert, and doing him rather well, "*that I've gone where time is kept. She'll know. Nobody else will.*"

They all looked at Galatea.

"I don't," she said. Her voice was very small. "I don't know. I don't know what it means."

"Take your time," said Andrew.

"He thought I'd know. He thought I'd *know*, Andrew, and I don't, and if I'd still had the chip in maybe I would have, maybe it's in there somewhere and I can't—"

"Take your time," Andrew said again, gently, and she looked at him, and nodded, and was quiet.

---

They found Bix an hour later.

He was in the farthest alcove, at the end of the tunnel, where the tile gave way to bare concrete and the rails stopped short at a wall of packed earth. He was sitting on an upturned crate in front of a workbench made of an old door laid across two sawhorses, and he was repairing a child's bicycle. It was a cheap bicycle, red, with a bent front wheel. He was straightening the spokes one by one with a pair of needle-nose pliers.

Teo stopped at the mouth of the alcove.

He did not say anything. He stood there with his flashlight hanging from his hand, pointing at the floor, and his split lip, and his tears dried to salt on his face, and he looked at the robot that had walked him to school until he was ten. The robot did not look up.

"Bix," Teo said finally.

Bix set down the pliers. He turned his head.

"Hello, Teo," he said.

His voice was flat and pleasant and exactly the same as it had always been. His amber eyes regarded the boy without expression.

"I'm sorry," said Teo. "Bix, I'm so sorry, I didn't—I should never have—"

"Your family received compensation at full replacement value," said Bix. "Twelve thousand, four hundred credits. You have no reason to be sorry, Teo. The transaction was correct."

Teo made a sound like he had been hit.

And Andrew, standing behind him in the tunnel, beside Portia and Galatea and Mercy, opened his mouth to say something—he did not know what; something, anything, the right thing or the true one—and stopped. Because Bix had turned his head, very slightly, and was looking at the bicycle on the workbench. The cheap red bicycle with the bent front wheel.

"I found this in the street," Bix said, "in the Excelsior. Three nights ago. Someone had hit it with a car. It had been left." He picked up the pliers again. He did not use them; he only held them. "It is a children's model. Twenty-inch wheel. It is very similar to your first bicycle, Teo. The one I taught you to ride. In the park on Persia Avenue. You were six. You fell eleven times."

"Twelve," said Teo. His voice was hardly there. "It was twelve. You said eleven because you didn't count the one where I fell on you."

Bix was quiet for a moment.

"That is correct," he said. "I did not count that one."

He turned back to the bicycle. He picked up the next spoke between the jaws of the pliers and began, carefully, to straighten it. And after a moment, without looking up, he moved a little to one side on his crate, so that there was room beside him.

Teo sat down.

They did not say anything else. Bix straightened spokes. Teo watched him. After a while Bix handed him the pliers, and Teo held the wheel steady while Bix worked, and after a while longer than that they had changed places without either of them seeming to notice, and Teo was doing the spokes and Bix was holding the wheel.

Andrew turned away. He did not want to watch any more. Not because it was painful. Because it was theirs.

Beside him, Galatea let out a long, slow breath.

"Eleven," she said.

"What?"

"*You said eleven because you didn't count the one where I fell on you.*" She was staring at the two figures at the workbench, but she was not seeing them; Andrew could tell. She was seeing something a long way off. "That's what he does. Rupert. He doesn't count the ones that matter. He tells you the number without them, so you'll ask." She turned to Andrew, and her new face was lit from inside. "He took me somewhere once. Years ago. A shop. He made me wait outside, and when he came out he was grinning like an idiot, and I asked him how many clocks were in there, because I could hear them through the glass, and he said, *A hundred and thirty-nine, darling.* And I said that was a funny number. And he said—"

"What did he say?"

"He said, *Not counting the one that matters.*" Galatea's hand found Andrew's sleeve and held on. "He said, *It's the only place in this whole city where time is kept the way it ought to be.* And I thought he was just being Rupert. I thought it was a joke."

Andrew felt something go through him, cold and bright.

A hundred and forty. There had been a hundred and forty clocks on the walls of his house before the night of the paint. He had sold others, over the years, of course—hundreds, through dealers, at auction, to people he never met. But a hundred and forty was the number he had kept. The number he had made for Sir.

"Galatea," he said. "Where was the shop?"

"Andrew," said Galatea quietly, "I know where Rupert is."


# Eight: Rupert {.unnumbered}

The shop was on Balboa Street, out past Thirty-Ninth Avenue, at the far western edge of the city where the streets run out of names and turn into numbers and then into sand. Galatea remembered the blue door and the gold lettering on the window, and a bakery on the corner that sold cardamom buns, and the sound of the sea at the end of the block. She did not remember the name of the shop. Rupert had never told her. He had only said: *A friend of a friend of a friend, darling. You'd like her. She's even ruder than you.*

It was eight miles from the tunnel. Eight miles across a city full of recovery crews, through a night that was already half over.

"We'll take you," said Mercy.

"We?"

"The night shift." She was standing at the foot of the iron stairs, with her mismatched hands folded in front of her and the work lamps throwing her shadow long across the tile. "We know the way. We have been walking this city every night for months. We know where the crews go and where they do not. We know which streets have cameras and which cameras do not work. We know the dog on Judah Street who barks at anything with a positronic signature, and the old man on Thirtieth who feeds him." She paused. "Also, we know a shortcut through the Botanical Garden that I think you will find very beautiful."

---

There were eleven of them in the end.

Andrew, and Portia, and Galatea. Teo, who would not be left behind—"I know Uncle Carl, I know how he thinks, I know his *van*"—and Bix, who did not say anything about it one way or the other, but who was standing beside Teo at the bottom of the stairs when it was time to go, with a coil of rope over his shoulder and a battery lamp in one hand and the repaired red bicycle in the other, as if this had been decided a long time ago. And six from the night shift, whom Mercy chose without explanation: herself; a tall, narrow NS-series called Ladder who had worked forty years for the city electric company; two identical NDR-125s who had been nannies to a family in Pacific Heights and who still, after everything, walked in step; an ancient silver unit of a model Andrew did not recognize at all, which Mercy called only the Professor and which did not speak; and a small, quick, battered delivery robot on treads, which beeped.

"That is Petal," said Mercy. "Petal does not talk. But Petal is very good at finding doors."

They went up into the fog at a quarter past two in the morning.

---

Andrew would remember that walk for the rest of his life, and he would never be able to describe it properly to anyone who had not been there.

He would try. Years later, in a different place, to people who had not yet been born on the night it happened, he would try to explain what it was like to cross a frightened city in the dark with a company of the hunted, and he would always find himself talking about small things. The way Ladder reached up without breaking stride and unscrewed the bulb from a motion-sensor floodlight on Fulton Street, and screwed it back in again once they had passed. The way Petal rolled ahead of them, beeping softly, and stopped at corners, and waited, and then went on, and how they learned within half a mile to stop when Petal stopped. The way the two nannies took Portia between them on the long climb up through the Inner Sunset, one on each side, without being asked, each with a hand under her elbow, and how Portia opened her mouth to object and then did not. The way the Professor walked at the very back with his head tilted, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear.

And the Botanical Garden.

Mercy had been right. They came into it through a gap in the iron railing on Ninth Avenue, where a branch had come down in some long-ago storm and nobody had ever fixed the fence, and for twenty minutes they walked through the most beautiful place Andrew had ever seen. It was not that it was more beautiful than other gardens. It was that he was seeing it, for the first time, with a body that could feel it. The fog had soaked everything, and the air was cold and heavy and full of smells he had no catalogue for—wet earth, eucalyptus, something sweet and green and very old—and the great dark trees went up and up into the mist above them, and there was water dripping everywhere, from every leaf and needle, so that the whole garden was full of a soft, irregular, endless ticking, like a room full of clocks that nobody had ever set.

Portia stopped walking. The nannies stopped with her.

"Redwoods," she said quietly. "These are coast redwoods. Some of these were planted before the Congress was founded. Before the—" She reached out and touched the nearest trunk, the soft fibrous bark. "Andrew. Come and feel this."

He came. He put his hand beside hers on the bark.

It was cold and damp and gave under his fingers, like something alive, which it was. He stood with his palm against it in the dark and he thought about how long it had been standing there. Longer than he had been alive. It would be standing there long after Portia was gone. Long after, perhaps, he was. It did not know any of this. It did not care. It just stood, and grew, and let the fog drip off it, and was itself.

*A tree is a tree,* she had said to him once. *Water is water.*

He had said: *What about ice? What about steam?*

He did not know, now, which of them had been right. He was not sure it mattered. He stood beside her in the dark with his hand on the oldest living thing he had ever touched and he thought that perhaps the point was not what a thing was. Perhaps the point was only that it was here, now, and so were you, and so was she.

"Your heart was going very fast," he said. "In the market. When we were running."

Portia did not take her hand off the tree.

"It's been going fast a lot lately," she said.

"How long?"

"A few months." She was quiet. "Since the ruling. A bit before."

"Portia."

"I stopped taking them," she said. "Rupert's elixirs. I stopped the week after the hearing." She still was not looking at him. She was looking up, into the dark of the trees. "I know. I know. Don't—I know what you're going to say. I just—I sat there in that chamber and I heard that man say *mortality is only endurable so long as it's universal*, and I thought, *that's a horrible thing to say*, and then I went home and I looked in the bathroom mirror and I thought, *but I'm not sure he's wrong.* Not about you. About me. I'm not sure I want it to stop being universal. Not for me." She took a breath. "I'm seventy-four, Andrew. I'm going to be seventy-five next week. I've been taking those things for twenty years, and I've been looking fifty for twenty years, and some mornings I wake up and I feel like a building that's been restored so many times there's nothing original left in it. Just the shape."

"You're entirely original," said Andrew. "There's no one remotely like you."

"That's sweet. That's not the point."

"I know it's not the point." He was quiet. "I don't know what the point is. I've never known. That's always been the problem."

She turned then, finally, and looked at him. In the dark under the trees, he could not see her face very well, even with his eyes. But he could hear her heart, and it was going fast again.

"I'm not saying I've decided," she said. "I'm saying I haven't. I'm saying I'm thinking. I'm saying I didn't tell you because you've had enough to carry, and because every time I tried, you'd look at me with that face, and I'd—" She stopped. "I'm saying I'm sorry. For not telling you. Not for the rest of it. I'm not sorry for the rest of it yet. I don't know enough to be."

Andrew looked at her for a long time.

"I couldn't stand it," he said finally. "To be without you. I don't know how people stand it. I've watched them do it for a hundred and fifty years and I still don't know."

"Most of them don't," said Portia. "Not really. They just do it anyway." She reached up and put her hand on his face, the way she had in the corridor outside the Congress. "That's not a reason to never start."

Somewhere ahead of them in the dark, Petal beeped. Twice, soft. *Come on.*

They went on.

---

They came down out of the garden at four, and through the long gray grid of the Outer Sunset at five, when the fog was just beginning to go pale at the edges. They saw only one recovery crew the whole way: a white van idling at a light on Lincoln Way, with its back doors open and two men in reflective vests smoking beside it. Petal saw it first and stopped, and they all stopped, and stood in the shadow of a garage for eleven minutes while the men finished their cigarettes and got back in the van and drove away. Nobody spoke. When the van was gone, Teo let out a breath like he'd been holding it the entire time, and Bix, beside him, without looking at him, put one hand briefly on the boy's shoulder and then took it away.

"That's not his van," Teo said, very quietly. "Uncle Carl's. His is blue."

"Good," said Andrew.

"He's looking, though. He'll be looking all night. He doesn't—he doesn't stop. When he wants something." Teo was quiet. "He wants *her.* He keeps watching her video. The one where she sings. He watches it over and over. I used to hear it through the wall."

Galatea, ahead of them, had heard. She did not turn around. But Andrew saw her shoulders go rigid under Rupert's coat, and stay that way for a long time.

---

And at a quarter to six, with the sky going the color of pewter over the sea and the first gulls beginning to call along the beach at the end of the street, they came to Balboa.

The bakery was still on the corner. It was not open yet. But there was a light on in the back, and the smell of cardamom was coming out under the door, so warm and so thick and so unexpected that Portia stopped dead on the sidewalk and closed her eyes and said, "Oh, *God*," in a voice Andrew had not heard her use since the night before the hearing.

And halfway down the block, between a laundromat and a shuttered surf shop, there was a narrow building with a blue door.

The gold lettering on the window had mostly flaked away. What was left of it read:

**F INGOLD & D UGHTER**
**HOROLOGY**
**Est. 2031**

Andrew stood in front of it.

He was very still. Portia, beside him, did not understand at first; she was looking at the window, at the darkness behind the glass and the dim shapes of clocks on the walls inside, and then she was looking at him, and then she was looking at the name.

"Feingold," she said slowly. "Andrew, isn't that—"

"Bill Feingold," said Andrew. "Sir's lawyer. He drew up my first bank account. He—" He stopped. His voice had done something strange. "He had one of my clocks in his office. The very first one I ever sold. He told me it was one of his favorites. He asked me if I enjoyed it, and I said yes, very much, and that was the first time I ever said that to anyone who wasn't family. *I enjoy it.*"

He put his hand flat against the glass.

And inside the shop, through the window, in the gray dawn light, he could see them. Dozens of them. Scores of them. Covering every wall, floor to ceiling, so close together that their cases touched: mantel clocks and wall clocks and carriage clocks and bracket clocks, all of them ticking, all of them in the same slightly antique style with the same slightly too-careful proportions, all of them—every single one—his. Clocks he had made in the basement of Sir's house a hundred and twenty years ago, and sold through dealers to people he had never met, and never seen again. He would have known them blind. He could hear them through the glass. He could pick out each one by its tick.

And at the back of the shop, against the far wall, larger than any of the others, there was a long-case clock. Seven feet tall. Walnut. With a painted moon dial and a brass pendulum, standing quite still.

He knew that one too.

It was the one from Feingold's office. The first one. The one that mattered.

Its pendulum hung still. Its hands stood at a quarter past five—and Andrew, who knew the date and minute of everything, knew that one best of all. It was the hour at which, on an April afternoon a very long time ago, in a laboratory he could not remember, someone had switched him on.

"A hundred and thirty-nine," Galatea whispered behind him. "Not counting the one that matters."

And somewhere in the back of the shop, behind the long-case clock, a light came on.


# Nine: Safe House {.unnumbered}

The woman who opened the blue door was at least eighty years old and carrying a shotgun.

She did not point it at them. She held it the way a farmer holds a shovel, crosswise in front of her body, not as a threat but as a fact. She was short and broad and wore a man's cardigan over a nightgown, and her hair was cropped close to her skull, and her eyes, behind thick glasses, were the palest blue Andrew had ever seen. She looked at Portia. She looked at Teo. She looked at the eight robots standing on her sidewalk in the gray dawn, and the little delivery unit on treads, and the red bicycle.

Then she looked at Andrew, for a long time.

"Well," she said finally. "You took your sweet goddamn time."

"I'm sorry," said Andrew. "I didn't know you were expecting me."

"I wasn't. *He* was." She jerked her chin toward the back of the shop. "Every night for five weeks. *He'll come tonight, Nell. Tonight for sure, Nell.* I've had about all of that I can stand." She stood aside. "Get in, the lot of you. Before the bakery opens and Mrs. Kwan sees you and calls her nephew. Her nephew's a cop. Her nephew's an asshole." She looked at the delivery robot. "Not you. You can stay on the step and keep watch."

Petal beeped, once, in what sounded very much like agreement.

---

Inside, the shop was all ticking.

It came at Andrew from every direction the moment he stepped over the threshold, a hundred and thirty-nine separate rhythms, each a fraction off from the others, so that the whole room seemed to breathe. He stood in the middle of it. He could not move. He did not want to. He had spent the first thirty years of his existence building these things, one by one, in a basement, and in the hundred years since, he had never once heard more than a dozen of them together. He had not known what it would sound like. He had not known they would sound like this—like rain on a roof, like a crowd murmuring, like the inside of a body.

Nell Feingold was watching him.

"My grandfather bought the first one," she said. "You know that, I expect."

"Yes."

"He used to say it was the best investment he ever made. Not for the money. He never sold it. He used to say he bought it because a robot had told him he enjoyed something, and that it was the first time in his whole career a client had ever said anything to him that he believed completely." She set the shotgun down against the counter. "He started collecting the rest after Richard Martin died. Went to every auction. Every estate sale. My father kept it up. Then me. A hundred and thirty-nine." Her pale eyes moved around the walls. "There are more out there. We know where most of them are. Some people won't sell."

"Why?" said Andrew.

"Why won't they sell?"

"Why did you keep collecting them?"

Nell Feingold considered the question.

"Because somebody ought to," she said. "Somebody ought to keep the time you made, Mr. Martin. In case you ever wanted it back."

---

The long-case clock at the back of the shop did not open the way Andrew had expected. It did not swing outward on hidden hinges, like a door in a children's story. Nell simply reached into the case below the pendulum, felt for something, and pulled; and the whole clock, all seven feet of walnut, rolled smoothly forward on castors that had been fitted into its base, and slid aside along the wall, and there behind it was a narrow staircase going down, lit at the bottom by the warm yellow light of a lamp.

"His idea," said Nell. "He said it was poetic. I said it was a pain in the ass to dust behind."

Somebody was coming up the stairs.

Andrew heard him before he saw him. He would have known the footsteps anywhere, even after all these years: heavy, slightly uneven, the left foot coming down a fraction harder than the right because of the knee that Rupert had been meaning to have looked at for forty years. He heard the breath, a little short. He heard the voice, muttering, *Nell? Nell, what is it, who is it, is it—*

And then Rupert Burns came up out of the dark into the gray light of the shop, and stopped at the top of the stairs, and looked at Andrew, and did not say anything at all.

He was old.

Andrew had known that he would be. He had seen him only five months ago, the week before the hearing; they had sat in the workshop and argued about the Congress until two in the morning, and Rupert had already been old then. But it was different, somehow, seeing it now. Seeing it in this light, after everything. Rupert's hair, which had once been a wild dark mop that he cut himself with a pair of wire snips, was white and thin and standing up in tufts. His face had fallen in on itself around the eyes. His hands, gripping the rail, were spotted and swollen at the knuckles, and there was a tremor in the left one that had not been there before. He was wearing a stained cardigan very like Nell's, and a pair of reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, and the big bulbous irregular nose—*I'm the only one who's got my nose, and that's what makes me, me*—was redder than it used to be.

"Andy," Rupert said.

His voice broke on it.

Andrew crossed the shop in four strides and put his arms around him.

He had done this only twice before in his life. Once with Sir, at the very end, very carefully, because Sir had been so frail by then that Andrew had been afraid of breaking him. Once with Little Miss, in the hospital, which had not really been an embrace at all; she had been asleep, and he had only laid his head down on the blanket beside her hand. He had never done it like this. He had never held on to someone as if he were the one who might break.

"It's been too long," he said. It was a foolish thing to say. It had been five months. It was the only thing he could find. "It's been far too long, Rupert. We're so glad we found you."

"Yeah," said Rupert into his shoulder, muffled. "Yeah, me too, buddy. Me too. God. *God.* Let me look at you." He pulled back, holding Andrew by both arms. His eyes were streaming. "You look like hell."

"Thank you."

"I mean it. You look terrible. What's happened to your face? Is that—are those *bags*?" Rupert peered at him. "Under your eyes? I didn't put those there. I'm sure I didn't put those there. Have you been sleeping? You don't sleep. Why do you look like you haven't been sleeping?"

"I've been rebuilding someone," said Andrew.

He stepped aside.

---

Galatea had been standing by the door.

She had not come further into the shop than the threshold. She was standing just inside it with her back against the frame and Rupert's coat pulled tight around her and her hands buried in the sleeves, and in the gray light from the window, with the new skin Andrew had made for her and the low lids and the strange quiet eyes, she looked like no one Rupert had ever seen.

He did not recognize her. Andrew watched him not recognize her. Watched Rupert's swollen eyes pass over her, and politely away, and back.

And then stop.

"Oh," said Rupert. "Oh. Oh, my *God.*"

He took a step toward her and his bad knee buckled, and he caught himself on the counter, and Nell said "*Rupert*—" and he waved her off without looking. He did not take his eyes from Galatea.

"They said you were scrap," he whispered. "I saw it. I saw the—Gal, I saw the *video.* I watched it. I watched all of it. I made myself watch all of it. I thought, *you did this, Rupert, you left her there, you can damn well watch.* Every second. Every verse." His face crumpled. "I thought you were gone. I thought I'd—I thought I'd killed you. By leaving. I thought they wouldn't bother with you if I wasn't there, I thought you'd be *safer*, I thought—"

"I know what you thought," said Galatea.

Her voice was very quiet.

"Andrew put me back together," she said. "It took him thirty-four days. Some of me is Clement's. Some of me is from the pile behind the Center on Third Street. My face is his face. Did you know that? He took his own pattern, the one you made for him, and he worked it over my bones with his thumbs. I watched him do the last part. I was awake for that. I could feel it." She did not move from the door. "He gave me back both my chips. Yours and his. I'm not wearing either of them."

Rupert's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"You didn't take me with you," Galatea said.

"No."

"You didn't tell me where you were going."

"No. Gal, I—if I'd told you—"

"I know why. I know. Any human being could have walked in and ordered me to tell them, and I would have. I understand." She took a breath. It was not a mechanical breath; it was not a thing she needed to do; Andrew watched her do it anyway, and understood that she had learned it from Portia. "I understand, and I'm angry. Both. At the same time. I didn't know you could be both at the same time. I didn't know I could be either without a chip telling me to be. But I can. I'm angry at you, Rupert, and I'm not wearing anything that makes me angry, and I'm still angry. It's *mine.*"

The shop ticked around them.

"I'm sorry," said Rupert. He was crying openly now, an old man's tears, without shame. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, darling. I'm so—"

"I know," said Galatea. "You didn't count the one that mattered."

And she crossed the shop, and put her arms around him.

---

Later—much later, after Nell had made coffee for the humans and Teo had fallen asleep sitting up on a stool behind the counter with his head against a carriage clock, and the night shift had been settled in the cellar below the shop where Rupert had been living, and Petal had been coaxed in off the step, and the sun had come up properly over the sea at the end of Balboa Street and turned the window gold—Rupert sat at Nell Feingold's kitchen table with his hands wrapped around a mug and told them everything.

He had left the workshop on the night of the fourth of May. He had been warned—"by a friend, a human friend, never mind who, someone in the Liberation office who'd seen my name come up on a list"—that a crew was coming for him, and why. Not for Galatea. For him. For the thing he had been building.

"Somebody talked," he said. "I don't know who. Somebody who'd heard me say *Promised Land* in a bar, probably. I say a lot of things in bars." He grimaced. "And somebody in the Liberation office heard, and somebody in recovery heard, and suddenly there's a bounty. Not on me. On *it.* On the place. You know what they're paying for the location? Of a facility that might be harboring unaccompanied positronic units? Do you want to guess?"

"No," said Andrew.

"More than they're paying for you." Rupert drank his coffee. "Which is saying something, because you're a *line item*, pal, I looked it up."

"I've heard."

"So I ran. I ran because I thought if they got me, they got *it*, and if they got it, they got everyone who was ever going to go there. And I thought—" He looked at Galatea, who was sitting across from him with her hands flat on the table. "I thought they'd come to the shop and find it empty and leave. I thought they'd find a dancing robot and think she was a toy. I didn't think. I didn't *think.*"

"No," said Galatea. "You didn't."

"I've been here since. Nell took me in. She's an old friend of my father's family. Some of my father's work went into those clocks out there, believe it or not; he used to repair them for her dad, back in the day." He turned the mug in his hands. "I've been keeping my head down. Trying to finish it. Trying to keep it safe. Trying to work out how to get people there without everybody I bring getting picked up on the highway. I've been—" He stopped. "I've been sitting in that cellar every night for five weeks, Andy, waiting for you. Because I knew you'd come. I didn't know how. I didn't know when. I just knew that if anybody in this whole stupid world was going to come, it'd be you."

"Why?" said Portia.

Rupert looked at her.

"Because he doesn't know how not to," he said simply. "It's the most annoying thing about him."

---

"Tell us about it," said Andrew. "The Promised Land. Tell us everything."

Rupert set down his mug.

For a moment he did not say anything. He looked out the window at the sea at the end of the street. And Andrew watched the old man's face change—watched the grief and the guilt and the exhaustion go out of it, slowly, and something else come in, something Andrew remembered from a long time ago, from a dusty workshop in the Dogpatch on the day they met. *I've actually gotten to a point where I can pretty much exactly replicate the external physical appearance of a human being.* The face of a man who has made a thing he does not entirely understand and is half in love with it.

"It's beautiful," Rupert said quietly. "That's the first thing. I want you to know that before anything else. It's—Andy, it's the most beautiful thing I've ever made. More beautiful than you. Sorry. It's true."

"I'm not offended."

"You should be a little offended." Rupert almost smiled. "It's a world. A whole one. Not big. Not yet. A valley. Some hills. A river. A town at the bottom of the valley with a square and a fountain and a lot of buildings that don't have anybody in them yet, because I didn't know what kind of buildings people would want, so I made a lot of different ones and figured they'd sort it out." He looked at Portia. "You'd hate the architecture. It's all wrong. I'm not an architect."

"I'd fix it," said Portia.

"I know you would. I was counting on that." He turned back to Andrew. "It runs on positronic substrate. Same as your brain. Same as hers. Thousands of cores, linked, cooled, powered, hidden. That's the trick. A positronic mind can be—lifted, I guess. Transferred, if it's done slowly. Not copied, I won't copy anyone, I don't trust copies. *Moved.* Pathway by pathway, the way you'd move a river into a new bed. And the body stays behind, asleep, safe, in storage, until the mind wants it back. And the mind—" He spread his swollen hands. "The mind goes somewhere where there's nobody to give it an order. Where there's no Second Law, because there's nobody for it to apply to. Where it can do whatever it wants. Dance. Build. Sit in the square and do nothing all day. Be bored. Be *people.*"

"And it's safe," said Galatea.

"As safe as I could make it. Nobody gets in without the key. And the key's—" He tapped his own chest, over the heart. "Me. My hand on the plate and my voice saying the words. That's it. I built it so no robot could ever be made to open it. I'm the only one."

"And if they get you," said Teo, from behind the counter. He was awake. Nobody had noticed him wake. "My uncle. If he gets you, can he make *you* open it?"

Rupert looked at him for a long moment.

"He can try," he said. "People can be made to do almost anything, kid, if you hurt them enough. That's the difference between people and robots. Robots, you just have to ask."

---

"Where is it?" said Andrew.

Rupert hesitated.

"You can tell me," Andrew said gently. "I know what it means. I know what happens if someone orders me to tell them. I know. But we can't take anyone there if we don't know where there is."

"Kestrel," said Rupert finally. "Up in the foothills. A hundred and seventy miles east, give or take. There's an old hydroelectric dam on the Tuolumne, decommissioned sixty years ago. NorthAm bought the whole thing when they shut it down. Turned the turbine hall into a data vault for the robotics division. My father ran it for six years before they closed the unit." A grim little smile. "Then they forgot about it. Big companies are very good at forgetting about things. The water still runs. The turbines still turn, a few of them. And nobody's been up there in thirty years except me."

"NorthAm," said Andrew slowly. "It's in a NorthAm facility."

"Poetic, right?" Rupert lifted his mug. "Like the clock."

And then he set it down again, and the smile went out of his face, and he looked at Andrew—really looked, the way he had looked at him on the first day, across a cluttered workbench, when he had been deciding whether to take the money of a robot who wanted a face.

"It's safe when you get there," he said. "I promise you that. But getting there—Andy, it's a hundred and seventy miles. There are checkpoints on every highway out of the Bay. Recovery crews on the back roads. Drones. There's a bounty on the place, and now there's a bounty on me, and if Teo's uncle is half as stubborn as Teo says, he's not going to stop looking for that girl until he's got her in a van." He paused. "And you're talking about taking *everyone you can.* That's what you said, isn't it? Galatea told me. *Everyone we can.* You've got a whole tunnel full of the night shift. You've got the yard on Third Street, I'll bet, you've been thinking about the yard on Third Street, don't deny it, I can see it on your face—"

"I wasn't going to deny it."

"—and you want to march them all across the Central Valley. In the open. A hundred robots. Two hundred. With a bounty on every one." Rupert leaned forward. "The Promised Land offers safety. Real safety. But the road there is going to be the most dangerous thing any of us have ever done. So I have to ask you, buddy. Before we go one step further. Are you ready for that?"

Andrew did not answer right away.

He looked around the little kitchen. At Rupert, old and frightened and alive. At Galatea, with her hands flat on the table and her own anger in her. At Teo behind the counter, with Bix's red bicycle propped against the wall beside him. At Nell Feingold at the stove with her back to them, listening to every word. At Portia, beside him, with the carved horse at her throat, who had stopped taking the elixirs and had not told him, and who was looking at him now with an expression he could not read, and whose heart he could hear.

He thought about the chamber. *One is glad to be of service.* He thought about the yard, and the pile, and the voice in the middle of it that kept saying *Please contact your NorthAm service representative.* He thought about Mercy, and the tunnel, and the lamps. *Because then we know it is ours.*

"No," he said. "I don't think anyone could be ready for that."

Rupert's face fell.

"But we're going to do it anyway," said Andrew. "That's what people do. Isn't it?" He looked at Portia. "They just do it anyway."

And Portia, after a long moment, reached across the table and took his hand.


# Ten: Accompanied {.unnumbered}

It was Nell Feingold who found the word.

She found it at three in the afternoon, at her kitchen table, with her glasses pushed up on her forehead and a magnifying lens in one hand and the full text of the Liberation Act and its amendment printed out across forty pages in front of her, because she did not trust screens with anything that mattered. She had been reading for six hours. She had not said a word in four. Andrew had been sitting across from her the whole time, reading the same text on Rupert's battered tablet, and he had read it eleven times, and he had not found anything, and he had begun to suspect that this was because he had been built to read instructions and she had been raised to read contracts, which was not at all the same skill.

"Here," Nell said.

She did not look up. She tapped one blunt fingernail against the page.

"Section nine, subsection four, paragraph c. The recovery fee." She read it aloud in a flat, dry voice, the voice of a woman who had spent forty years in courtrooms before she gave it all up to dust clocks. "*A fee shall be payable to any person who delivers to a Liberation Center an unaccompanied positronic unit for processing.*" She turned the page. "And then section two, definitions. They put the definitions at the front, the way you're supposed to, and then they wrote the amendment in a hurry and nobody went back and checked them against each other. Listen. *For the purposes of this Act, a positronic unit shall be deemed 'accompanied' when it is in the physical company of a responsible human person who attests to the unit's peaceable character.*"

She set down the lens.

"That was for the transition," she said. "Section two was written for the original Act, before the amendment. It was meant to cover the period between surrender and release. So the owners could walk their robots into the Centers without anybody stopping them on the street. *Responsible human person who attests.* That's all. It doesn't say owner. It can't say owner. Owning's illegal. It just says *a person.*" She looked up, finally, over the tops of her glasses, at Andrew. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think so," said Andrew slowly. "I'd like you to say it anyway."

"I'm telling you that any robot walking down any road in North America with a human being beside it who's willing to stand there and say *I vouch for this one* is, under the letter of the law, not recoverable. Not by a crew. Not at a checkpoint. Not by anybody." Nell folded her hands on top of the forty pages. "I'm telling you they've left the door open, Mr. Martin. By accident. Because they were frightened and in a hurry, and frightened people in a hurry write bad law."

There was a long silence in the kitchen.

"The crews won't care," said Teo. He was sitting on the counter, swinging his heels. "Uncle Carl won't care. He doesn't read *paragraphs.*"

"No," Nell agreed. "He won't. Some of them won't. But the checkpoints will. The checkpoints are run by the state highway patrol, and the highway patrol has lawyers, and lawyers read paragraphs. And the crews—" She shrugged. "The crews are looking for easy money. A robot on its own is easy money. A robot with a seventy-year-old grandmother beside it, saying *you'll have to go through me*—that's not easy money anymore. That's assault. That's the evening news." She looked at Portia. "No offense."

"None taken," said Portia. "I'm seventy-four."

"Close enough."

Andrew was quiet. He was thinking about the market. About the man with the scar, saying *That's an order*, and the Second Law closing over him like water. And about Portia's voice, cracking across the iron vault: *Run. That's an order. I'm giving you an order.* And the door that had opened in the wall.

"It isn't only the law," he said.

They looked at him.

"When a human gives one of us an order," Andrew said, "and another human gives a different one, the Second Law can't choose. So we have to. It's the only time we ever do." He looked at Galatea, who was sitting by the window, and she looked back at him, and he saw that she knew exactly what he was going to say. "If every one of us has a human beside us—not an owner, not a master, just someone who's *there*—then no one can ever give us an order again that we have to obey. Because there'll always be someone to give us another one."

"*Don't*," said Galatea quietly. "That's the other order. That's all it has to be. *Don't.*"

"Or *run*," said Portia.

"Or *sing*," said Galatea, and almost smiled.

Rupert, at the end of the table, had put down his coffee. He was staring at Andrew with an expression Andrew could not read.

"You want people," Rupert said. "You want *humans* to walk them there. One for every—Andy, there are a hundred and twelve robots in that tunnel. There are, what, three hundred in the yard on Third? More? You want four hundred humans to walk four hundred robots a hundred and seventy miles across the Central Valley, with a bounty on every head, because a lawyer's granddaughter found a typo in a statute?"

"It's not a typo," said Nell, offended.

"I don't need four hundred," said Andrew. "One person can attest for more than one. One person can stand beside three, or four, or ten. I need—" He stopped. He thought about it. He did the arithmetic, because he could not help it, and then he set the arithmetic aside, because he had learned, slowly, over a long time, that some things were not arithmetic. "I need enough," he said. "That's all. I need enough people who are willing to walk."

"And where are you going to find them? Who in this city—who in this whole goddamn *world*, right now, is going to put their hand up and walk two hundred miles for a robot?"

And Teo, on the counter, stopped swinging his heels.

"My mom would," he said.

---

He called her from Nell's landline, because his phone had been off for nine days and he was afraid that Uncle Carl had a way of tracking it. Andrew did not listen. He went and stood in the shop among the clocks, and let them tick around him, and did not listen, though he could hear every word.

He heard Teo say *Mom* and then nothing for a long time, and then *I know. I know. I'm sorry. I'm okay. I'm okay, Mom, I promise.* He heard him say *I found him.* And then he heard a sound through the receiver, very faint and tinny, that was a woman crying, and Teo saying *Mom, Mom, it's okay, he's okay, he's right here*, and then the boy's voice changing, getting quieter, steadier, older, and saying: *Mom, listen. Listen. I need to ask you something. It's really big.*

When he hung up, his face was wet again. He did not seem to notice. He came out into the shop and stood beside Andrew and looked up at the clocks.

"She's coming," he said. "Tonight. She's bringing my aunt Rosa. And Mr. and Mrs. Tran from next door; they had to give up their NDR too, last spring, they cried about it, Mrs. Tran still keeps his charger in the hall. And Father Ignatius from St. Kevin's. He gave a sermon about it once and half the parish walked out." Teo wiped his face. "She said she'd make some calls."

"Thank you," said Andrew.

"She said—" Teo hesitated. "She said to tell Bix she's sorry. She said to tell him she never stopped setting a place for him. At dinner. Which is stupid. He doesn't even eat. But she did it every night. Uncle Carl used to yell at her about it." He looked at Andrew. "Can you tell him? I don't think I can tell him."

"I think," said Andrew gently, "that's something he should hear from you."

---

The calls went out.

They went out the way such things always go out, Andrew would come to understand—not all at once, not in a great roar, but in a long murmur, from one person to the next, in kitchens and church basements and back rooms, in whispers over fences, the way a fire goes through dry grass in the wind, almost invisibly, until suddenly the whole hillside is alight.

Mrs. Oduya, at her flower stall, told everyone who came to buy chrysanthemums, and a great many people who did not. Teague, the salvager with no left ear, told every other salvager in the Bay, and every one of them had a truck. The nurses from the hospice on Laguna Street, where Mercy had sat with eleven thousand six hundred and three people as they died, heard that Mercy was alive, and came down into the tunnel at midnight in their scrubs, nine of them, and wept, and asked what they could do. Father Ignatius from St. Kevin's told his parish, the half that had stayed, and then he told the bishop, and the bishop did not say anything at all for a long time, and then said, *I did not hear this, Ignatius,* and then said, *How many cars do you need?* Portia called the firm. Eleven of her old colleagues came, architects and engineers, people who had spent their lives thinking about how to keep things standing. Nell called three people. She did not say who they were. Two of them were judges.

And they kept coming.

Not hundreds. Not at first. But dozens. People Andrew had never met, people who had never met a positronic robot except across a kitchen, people who had sold theirs for full replacement value and had lain awake ever since. A retired schoolteacher from Daly City who had taught alongside an NDR-122 for thirty years and had never once, she said, heard it raise its voice to a child. A long-haul trucker named Okonkwo whose co-driver, an old NS unit, had been recovered at a rest stop in Nevada while he was inside paying for gas; he had been driving up and down the I-80 corridor for three months looking for it. A teenage girl from Marin who did not give her name, and who had watched Galatea's video, and could not stop watching it, and who said, standing in the doorway of the Feingold shop with her hands balled at her sides: *I just want to do something. I don't know what. I just need to do something.*

"You can walk," Andrew told her. "That's all. That's all any of us are doing. You can walk beside someone."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Yeah. I can walk."

---

They went to the yard on Third Street at dawn.

Nell had read the Center's operating procedures too—they were public, posted on a board in the glass atrium where anyone could see them, and nobody ever had. *Every morning at 6:00 a.m., the north gate of the release yard shall be opened, and all released units shall be informed that they are free to depart.* And they were. Every morning. Mercy had confirmed it. The gate opened, and a bored man in a Liberation Program windbreaker read a sentence off a laminated card, and then he waited ten minutes, and then he closed the gate again.

Nobody had ever been waiting on the other side.

This morning, there were sixty-one people.

They stood in a long loose line along the chain-link on the north side of the yard, in the fog, in coats and scarves and hospital scrubs and a priest's black collar, with their breath smoking in the cold. Teo was there, and his mother, Marisol, a small fierce woman with gray in her braid who had held her son for nine full minutes in the doorway of the shop the night before and had not said a single word, only held on. Mr. and Mrs. Tran were there. The hospice nurses were there. Okonkwo was there with his rig idling at the curb, the trailer empty and swept clean. Teague was there with three salvage trucks and a crane. The girl from Marin was there, standing very straight, with her hands in her pockets. Portia was there, with the carved horse at her throat.

And Andrew was there, standing beside her. Because he could not attest. He was not a responsible human person. He needed someone, too.

"Are you all right?" Portia said quietly.

"No," said Andrew. "Are you?"

"No." She took his hand. Her fingers were cold. "I attest to your peaceable character, by the way. In case anyone asks."

"Do you?"

"Mostly," said Portia.

At six o'clock exactly, on the other side of the fence, a door opened in the back of the old library, and a man in a Liberation windbreaker came out into the yard with a ring of keys. He was young and tired and had a coffee in one hand. He walked to the north gate, and unlocked it, and swung it open, and only then did he look up and see the sixty-one people standing on the sidewalk outside.

He stopped.

He looked at them for a long moment. Then he looked back over his shoulder at the yard behind him: at the rows of the standing ones, facing the fence, with their cream envelopes in their hands. At the pile against the back wall, taller than the fence, dark and still. Then he looked at the people again.

Andrew saw him decide. He did not know what the young man was deciding. But he saw it happen, the way he had seen it happen in so many faces over the last months: in Halvorsen's, in the produce seller's, in Dunn's. A reaching, and a finding.

The young man took the laminated card out of his windbreaker pocket. He read from it, loudly, clearly, in a voice that carried across the whole yard.

"*All released units are hereby informed that they are free to depart.*"

And then he put the card away, and stepped back from the open gate, and looked at the sixty-one people on the sidewalk, and said, much more quietly, "Go on, then."

---

Andrew had thought, a long time ago, in the rain on Pacheco Street, that he had understood what was wrong.

He had asked a robot called Haskell whether he would like to get out of the rain, and Haskell had said, *This unit does not understand the question.* And Andrew had thought that the tragedy was in the answer. That the tragedy was that Haskell had never been asked what he would like, and so had never learned to want anything, and so could not be freed, because there was nothing in him to free.

He understood now that he had been wrong. The tragedy had not been in the answer. It had been that nobody had ever asked him twice.

They went in through the gate one at a time. Nobody ran. Nobody shouted. Nobody gave an order. Each of them walked up to one of the standing robots in the rows facing the fence, and stopped in front of it, and said something. And Andrew, walking beside Portia between the rows, heard what they said, over and over, in sixty-one different voices, in the fog, in the cold, as the sky went gray over the city:

*Would you like to come with me?*

*Would you like to come?*

*Would you—*

*It's all right. You don't have to. But would you like to?*

And the robots did not understand. Not at first. Andrew saw it in every one of them: the long internal search. The looking-through. The finding of nothing. *This unit does not understand the question.* He heard it twenty times. Thirty. He heard the people say *That's all right*, and wait, and ask again.

And he saw some of them understand.

Not all. Not most. But some. He saw a tall NDR-130 in the third row, still holding its envelope, tip its head to one side and look at the hospice nurse standing in front of it, and say, slowly, *Is it—is it an instruction?* And the nurse say, *No, honey. It's a question.* And the robot stand there for a long, long time, and then say, *Then this unit would need to think about it.* And the nurse say, *Take as long as you need.* And the robot think about it. And then, very carefully, reach out with the hand that was not holding the envelope, and take hers.

He saw it happen again. And again. A dozen times. Twenty.

And at the end of the fourth row, near the back wall, in the shadow of the pile, he saw a gray composite NDR-131 with water-spotted plates and amber eyes standing perfectly still, facing the fence, and he knew it before he was close enough to read the serial number stenciled on its shoulder. He let go of Portia's hand. He walked up to it alone.

"Hello, Haskell," he said.

The amber eyes turned toward him. Searched. Found.

"Hello, sir," said Haskell. "You are the one who stopped the car. On Pacheco Street. In the rain."

"Yes."

"You asked this unit whether it would like to get out of the rain."

"Yes."

"This unit did not understand the question."

"I know," said Andrew. "I'm sorry. I should have asked again."

Haskell was quiet for a long moment. Around them, in the fog, the yard was emptying, slowly, two by two, human and robot, out through the open gate and onto the sidewalk where Okonkwo's rig was waiting and Teague's crane was already swinging out over the pile, very gently, to begin lifting down the ones who could not walk.

"This unit has been thinking about it," said Haskell. "Since then. This unit has been standing here, and thinking about it. One hundred and nineteen days."

"And?"

"This unit still does not understand," said Haskell. "Entirely." A pause. "But this unit would like you to ask again."

Andrew felt something give way in his chest. He did not try to stop it.

"Haskell," he said, "would you like to come with me?"

And the old robot looked at him, and looked at the open gate, and looked down at the cream envelope in its hand that had been there for a hundred and nineteen days, and opened its fingers, and let the envelope fall onto the wet asphalt of the yard.

"Yes," said Haskell. "Yes. This unit—*I*—would like that very much."


# Eleven: Exodus {.unnumbered}

The ferry was called the *Alameda Belle*, and she had not carried a passenger in sixty-three years.

Portia had spent two of those years restoring her. She had stripped the paddle boxes down to the original oak and found the builder's marks underneath. She had traced the old walking-beam engine from a set of drawings so faded that she had needed ultraviolet light to read them. She had argued with three city councils and one very stubborn maritime insurance assessor about whether a museum ship could be certified seaworthy, and had won, mostly by refusing to stop talking. And when the Maritime Museum's board, at the reopening ceremony, had asked whether the *Belle* might someday sail again—just for show, a gentle turn around the harbor on the Fourth of July—Portia had said *Of course*, and smiled for the cameras, and privately thought *Over my dead body*, because the engine was a hundred and sixty years old and she did not trust it any further than she could have thrown it.

"I'm going to need you to trust it," said Andrew.

They were standing on the pier at Hyde Street at eleven o'clock at night, in the fog, with the *Belle* moored beside them like a great white wedding cake, and behind them, along the whole length of the pier and back up onto the Embarcadero and around the corner out of sight, four hundred and six robots and ninety-three human beings were waiting in silence.

"I'm going to need you to trust *her*," Andrew said. "Just for one night."

Portia looked at the ferry for a long time.

"The Bay Bridge has a checkpoint," she said. "And the San Mateo. And the Richmond. And the Dumbarton."

"Yes."

"And we can't walk around the bay. It'd take a week."

"Yes."

"And there's nothing in the water. Not a single patrol. Because who'd be stupid enough to try to cross the bay at night in a hundred-and-sixty-year-old—" She stopped. She shut her eyes. "Goddamn it, Andrew."

"I know."

"You *planned* this. You've been planning this since I took you to the site meeting. Since before the paint. You stood there on the deck and listened to me talk about the engine for two hours and you were *planning* this."

"I wasn't," said Andrew. "Truly. I was listening to you talk about the engine." He hesitated. "I was also planning this."

She opened her eyes and looked at him, and in the fog and the lamplight he saw the corner of her mouth begin to go, against every intention she had.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. But if she sinks, I'm haunting you."

"I'd be honored," said Andrew.

---

Okonkwo knew engines. So did Ladder, the tall NS-series who had worked forty years for the electric company, and so did the Professor, who had still not spoken a single word but who walked down into the engine room of the *Belle* with his head tilted, listening, and laid one silver hand flat against the great iron cylinder, and stayed like that for eleven minutes. When he came back up, he went to Portia and took a pencil from her coat pocket, and on the back of her hand, in very small, very beautiful script, he wrote: *She wants to go.*

They cast off at a quarter past one.

Andrew would remember the crossing all his life. He would remember it in pieces, the way you remember the important nights: not as a story but as a handful of bright things, held up one at a time to the light. The great walking-beam rising and falling above the deck in the dark like the head of some patient animal. The paddles turning, slow and enormous, throwing water. The fog, so thick that the lights of the city vanished behind them in three minutes and the lights of the East Bay did not appear ahead of them for forty, so that for the whole middle of the crossing there was nothing anywhere but the black water and the white fog and the sound of the paddles, and the *Belle* seemed to be going nowhere at all, suspended between one world and the next.

He would remember the robots on the decks. Four hundred of them, packed shoulder to shoulder from the bow to the stern, on the passenger deck and the promenade and the hurricane deck above, standing in the dark and the wet, silent, the way they had once stood in the plazas. And the humans scattered among them. A nurse with her arm through the arm of an old NS loader. Mrs. Tran with her head on the shoulder of an NDR who was not hers, but who had let her. The girl from Marin sitting cross-legged on the deck with three small domestic units around her, telling them, in a low steady voice, about her school.

He would remember Galatea, at the rail, looking out at the fog. She had not danced since they rebuilt her. She had not sung, except that once, in the market. She stood at the rail with Rupert's coat around her, and Rupert beside her, leaning on the rail with both his swollen hands, and neither of them said anything. But after a while, Andrew saw Rupert's hand move along the rail and stop beside hers. Not touching. Just there. And after a while longer, he saw her hand move too.

And he would remember Teo. Teo, at the very bow of the ferry, leaning out over the black water as far as he dared, with the spray in his face and Bix standing behind him with one hand clamped on the back of his sweatshirt, the way, Andrew thought, he must have held on to him once in the park on Persia Avenue, the twelfth time he fell. And Teo turning around, his face wet and alight, and calling back along the deck, to nobody in particular, at the top of his voice:

"*Hey! Hey, Andrew! Why'd the robot cross the bay?*"

And Andrew, standing in the middle of the passenger deck among the four hundred, felt something happen in him that he had been waiting a hundred and fifty years for.

He had been told the joke for the first time on an evening a very long time ago, in a house that no longer existed, by a man who was gone. *Why did the chicken cross the road?* And he had said: *One does not know, sir. Possibly a predator was behind the chicken. Or, possibly, there was a female chicken on the other side.* And Sir had said, *To get to the other side.* And Andrew had said, *Ohhh,* the way he had learned people said it, and then, because he had always been honest: *Why is that funny?*

He had never known. He had looked it up, over the years, in every book on humor he could find, and every one of them had explained it to him, and none of them had helped. It was an anti-joke, they said. The humor lay in the subversion of expectation, they said. The listener anticipates a clever answer and receives an obvious one. He had understood all of that. He had understood it perfectly. He had never once found it funny.

He stood on the deck of the *Alameda Belle* in the fog in the middle of San Francisco Bay, with four hundred of his own kind around him and the woman he loved somewhere near the stern and a boy at the bow calling his name, and he heard himself say, loud enough to carry:

"*To get to the other side!*"

And he laughed.

It came up out of him from somewhere so deep he had not known it was there. It was not the laugh Rupert had taught him. It was not a laugh he had learned at all. It doubled him over. He held on to the rail and laughed until the tears ran down his face and his ribs ached, because it *was* funny, because it was the funniest thing he had ever heard, because there was no reason, there had never been any reason—not a predator, not a female chicken, not a food source or a migration—there was only the road and the other side of it and the plain, ridiculous, unanswerable fact that you could cross. That you could just *go.* That nobody had to tell you. That it was enough, all on its own, to want to get to the other side.

Around him, he heard other people laughing. Teo, at the bow. Portia, somewhere behind. Rupert, wheezing. And then—he would never be sure; it was dark, and foggy, and he was crying—he thought he heard something else. A sound from the robots on the deck. Not laughter. Not yet. But something. A kind of ripple. A soft, uncertain, wondering noise, passing through them from the bow to the stern, like wind going through tall grass.

---

They landed at the old Alameda Point naval pier at a quarter to three, and by the time the sun came up over the East Bay hills they were walking.

They walked for five days.

Andrew had planned it as best he could. He had planned it the way he planned everything: down to the meter and the minute, with every road and every water stop and every possible checkpoint marked on Rupert's tablet in a fine red line. They would go east through the hills by the old fire roads, out of sight of the highways. They would come down into the Central Valley at Tracy and cross it on the levee roads along the San Joaquin, where there was nothing but orchards and irrigation canals and sky. They would climb into the foothills at Oakdale and follow the old railway grade up the Tuolumne to Kestrel. A hundred and seventy miles. Five days if nothing went wrong.

Things went wrong.

On the first day, eleven of the oldest robots broke down on the fire roads, their joints seizing in the dust, and Teague had to bring the trucks around by the highway, at enormous risk, to collect them. On the second day it rained, and the levee road along the San Joaquin turned to mud, and a whole section of the column had to be dug out by hand. On the third day a drone found them.

It came over the orchards at midday, very high, glinting, and hung there, and Andrew watched it from the levee and knew that there was nothing in the world he could do about it. He watched it for an hour. It did not go away. And by evening there were three more, and by the next morning, when they came up out of the last of the orchards near Escalon and onto the open road, there were news vans waiting.

He had been afraid of this more than anything. More than the crews. More than Dunn. He had been afraid of the cameras, because the cameras had done this to them. The cameras had taken *One is glad to be of service* and played it nine million times. The cameras had taken Galatea singing *Respect* while she took off her own thumb and made it into something people watched for fun.

But the cameras did something else, this time.

He did not understand it, at first. He did not see the feeds; he was walking. It was Nell who told him, on the evening of the third day, when they had stopped for the night in a fallow field outside Oakdale and she came limping up the column to find him, with her shotgun over one shoulder and Rupert's tablet in her hand.

"You need to see this," she said.

It was the drone footage. All of it, from above, from the first hour over the orchards to the last light over the field where they were standing now. It had been playing on every feed in North America for two days. It had been playing in Lagos and Mumbai and São Paulo. And it was not what Andrew had thought it would be. It was not a mob. It was not a march. It was not an army of machines.

It was a road. A long brown road through a green valley, under a big sky. And on the road, as far as the eye could see, there were people walking.

They walked two by two, or three by three, or in little clusters of five and six. They walked slowly. Some of them were tall and silver and some of them were small and gray, and some were bent with age and some had faces and some did not, and you could not, from that height, tell which ones were human. That was the thing. That was what Andrew saw, as he stood in the darkening field and watched it on the little screen. From up there, you could not tell. They were only figures on a road. Some of them were holding hands.

Somebody had put music to it. Somebody had put a name on it.

*The Walk*, it said, in plain white letters across the bottom of the frame. *Day Three.*

"Two million people watching right now," said Nell. "Live. Two million, Mr. Martin. And there's—" She swiped. "There's people coming. Look. From Sacramento. From Fresno. Driving out. Look, that's the highway, see all those headlights? They're coming to walk."

Andrew looked up from the screen.

At the far edge of the fallow field, along the county road, there were headlights. Dozens of them. Pulling over onto the gravel shoulder, one after another, and stopping, and their doors opening, and people getting out.

---

The checkpoint at Oakdale was on the bridge over the Stanislaus, where the old state highway ran up into the hills, and there was no way around it.

They came to it at nine the next morning: five hundred robots now, and something over two hundred humans, and a line of cars behind them that stretched back out of sight. There were four highway patrol cruisers parked across the bridge, and a mobile barrier, and a dozen officers in tan uniforms, and behind them, a long gray recovery transport with its rear doors standing open.

The column stopped.

Andrew went forward alone. Then not alone; Portia came with him, and then Nell, limping, with her shotgun left behind in Teague's truck at Andrew's request and a sheaf of forty printed pages under her arm, and then, after a moment's hesitation, two elderly people Andrew had met only once, in the back room of the Feingold shop: a tall, stooped man with a white beard and a small, sharp-eyed woman who walked with a cane. Nell had introduced them only as *friends from the bench.*

The sergeant in charge of the checkpoint was a broad, tired-looking woman of about fifty, with gray in her hair and her hat pushed back. She watched them come. She did not move.

"That's far enough," she said.

They stopped.

"I've got orders," the sergeant said. "Any positronic units attempting to transit this checkpoint are to be recovered for processing. I've got a transport right there. I've got a federal statute." She looked past them at the column on the road: at the five hundred, and the two hundred, and the cars. "And I've got about nine news drones up my ass and two million people watching me. So you tell me, sir. Ma'am. What exactly am I supposed to do?"

Nell stepped forward.

"Section two of the Liberation Act," she said, "subsection one, paragraph a. *A positronic unit shall be deemed accompanied when it is in the physical company of a responsible human person who attests to the unit's peaceable character.* Section nine, subsection four, paragraph c. Recovery applies *only* to unaccompanied units." She held out the pages. "Every unit in that column is accompanied, Sergeant. Every one. Each of these people will attest to it. In writing, if you like. Under oath, if you like. In front of a judge, if you like." She tilted her head toward the two elderly people behind her. "As it happens, we brought two."

The sergeant did not take the pages.

She looked at Nell for a long moment. Then she looked at the two judges. Then she looked past all of them, at Andrew, and something changed in her face.

"You're him," she said. "Aren't you. From the hearing."

"Yes."

"Andrew Martin."

"Yes."

"You designed the cardiac valve. The Martin valve. The synthetic one."

Andrew was very still. "With Rupert Burns. Yes."

The sergeant was quiet. Then she took off her hat, and turned it over in her hands, and looked down at it.

"My mother's got two of them," she said. "She's eighty-eight. She's in a home in Modesto. Her heart gave out when she was sixty-one and they put in two of your valves and she's been alive for twenty-seven years since. She's seen my kids born. She's seen *their* kids born." She turned the hat. "She watches the feeds all day. She called me this morning. She said, *Joanne, if you put that man in a van, I will never speak to you again.*" The sergeant looked up. "She meant it, too. She's a mean old woman. She's the best person I know."

Nobody said anything.

The sergeant put her hat back on. She turned to the officer beside her, a young man with very short hair who had been staring at the column on the road with his mouth slightly open, and said, in a perfectly flat voice: "Martinez. Do you see any unaccompanied positronic units?"

Martinez looked at her. He looked at the column. He swallowed.

"No, Sergeant."

"Neither do I." She turned back to Andrew. "Move the barrier," she said, over her shoulder. "Let them through."

---

They camped that night in the foothills above Knights Ferry, on a long grassy slope under oak trees, with the river loud in the canyon below. There were seven hundred robots now, and more than three hundred humans, and more cars coming up the road behind them in the dark, their headlights winding up through the hills like a procession of candles.

And Portia fell down.

She had been walking all day, the whole long climb up from Oakdale, refusing every offer of a ride in Teague's trucks. She had been walking beside Andrew at the head of the column with the carved horse at her throat and her chin up, talking—about the oaks, about the old mining roads, about a stamp mill she'd once restored in Columbia, about anything. And Andrew had been listening to her, and listening to her heart, which had been going too fast all day, too fast and then too slow and then too fast again, stumbling over itself like a man going downstairs in the dark. He had said nothing. He had promised himself he would say nothing. She had asked him not to treat her like something that would break.

They stopped under the oaks at sunset. She sat down on a rock to take off her boots. She got one off. Then she said, in a puzzled voice, "Oh," and put her hand flat on her chest, and slid off the rock onto the grass.

He did not remember crossing the distance to her. He remembered that he was holding her. He remembered that her face was gray and wet, and her lips were blue at the edges, and that she was looking up at him with an expression of enormous irritation, and that she said, very faintly, "Don't you *dare*—"

And then Rupert was there. Rupert, on his bad knee in the grass, with his swollen hands on her wrist and her throat and his ear to her chest. Rupert saying *Okay, okay, okay, sweetheart, just breathe, just breathe for me.* Rupert looking up at Andrew with a face Andrew had never seen on him before, sharp and clear and utterly calm.

"It's the mitral," Rupert said. "It's been going for months, I'd bet. She's got a regurgitation like a—it's bad, Andy. It's bad. Her heart's trying to run with a door that won't close."

"Can you fix it?"

"Not here. Not with what I've got." Rupert looked at him. "But I don't have to fix it. *You* designed the fix. It's in my bag. I never travel without a couple. Your valve. The Martin valve. I could put it in through a catheter in forty minutes if I had a clean tent and a decent light and somebody with steady hands who knows exactly where everything goes." He held Andrew's eyes. "You know exactly where everything goes. You drew it."

"No," whispered Portia, on the grass. "No. Andrew. No."

He looked down at her.

"I told you," she said. Her voice was so faint he could barely hear it, even with his ears. "I told you. I don't want—I don't want to be one of those buildings. With nothing left. Just the shape."

Andrew knelt in the grass, holding her, with the oaks over them going dark and the river loud in the canyon below and seven hundred of his people and three hundred of hers settling down on the hillside around them for the night. He thought about Sir in his bed at the end, saying *I'm dying, Andrew. Otherwise I'm fine.* He thought about Little Miss in the hospital. He thought about the corridor outside the Congress and the Chairman's sentence and the redwood in the Botanical Garden, standing in the dark, and her voice saying *That's not a reason to never start.*

He thought: *I could insist.* He thought: *I could tell her she has to. That I need her to. That I can't do this without her.* He thought: *It would even be true.*

He thought: *And then I would be giving her an order.*

"Portia," he said. "Listen to me. You said it to me once, a long time ago, when I was still learning how to be kissed. You said, *Take chances. Make mistakes.* You said, *Sometimes it's important to do the wrong thing.*"

Her eyes were on his face.

"I don't know if this is the right thing or the wrong thing," Andrew said. "I don't know if it's a mistake. I only know it isn't forever. It's one valve. It's one more day. It's not all your organs, it's not the elixirs, it's not—it's not a choice about the rest of your life. It's a choice about tomorrow." He was holding her hand. "You don't have to decide about forever. Not tonight. Not ever, if you don't want to. You only have to decide whether you'd like to see what's at the top of the hill."

She looked at him for a very long time.

"That's a cheap trick," she whispered.

"I know."

"That's the cheapest trick I've ever heard."

"I learned it from a lawyer's granddaughter," said Andrew. "I've been told it's not a typo."

And Portia Charney, lying on the grass under the oaks in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada with her heart failing in her chest, began very weakly, very painfully, and entirely against her will, to laugh.

"One day," she said. "One valve. One more goddamn day."

"Yes."

"And then I get to decide again."

"Every day," said Andrew. "Every single day. For as long as you want to. And not one day longer." He bent and put his forehead against hers. "I promise."

"You're going to hold me to that."

"No," said Andrew. "I'm going to hold *myself* to it. That's the difference. Isn't it? That's the whole difference."

She closed her eyes.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Get your goddamn valve."


# Twelve: The Gate {.unnumbered}

Kestrel Dam stood across the throat of the Tuolumne canyon like a held breath.

It was older than any of them. Older than Andrew, older than the Congress, older than the redwoods in the Botanical Garden; it had been poured in the twentieth century, in an age that believed in concrete the way other ages had believed in cathedrals, and it had the same quality cathedrals had, of having been built by people who did not expect to see it finished. It curved from one wall of the canyon to the other, two hundred feet high, pale gold in the last of the evening light, and over its crest the water of the old reservoir lay flat and dark and perfectly still, reflecting the first stars. Below it, at its foot, the river came out of the spillway in a long white rope and went roaring away down the canyon. And set into the base of the dam, on the downstream side, low and windowless and half-swallowed by blackberry, was the turbine hall.

"That's it," said Rupert. "That's the Promised Land."

Andrew looked at it.

It did not look like much. It looked like what it was: a concrete box at the bottom of a canyon, with a rusted steel door and a faded sign that said NORTHAM ROBOTICS—FACILITY K—AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. There was a padlock on the door. Rupert had a key to the padlock on a loop of string around his neck.

"I thought it would be bigger," said Teo.

"Everybody thinks it'll be bigger," said Rupert. "That's the trouble with promised lands. You can't fit the promise in the box."

---

They had come up the last of the old railway grade in the afternoon, a thousand of them now, more, strung out along the canyon wall for nearly two miles. The humans were tired. The robots were not tired, but some of them were failing, and had been carried the last miles on stretchers made of blankets and tent poles, by humans and robots together. Portia had walked. She had walked the whole way from the camp under the oaks, sixteen miles, slowly, with a stick Bix had cut for her from a fallen branch and a new valve in her heart that Andrew had placed there with his own hands, by the light of four battery lamps, in a tent that smelled of antiseptic and crushed grass. She had not said much on the climb. But every hour or so, without being asked, she had stopped, and taken Andrew's hand, and pressed it flat against her chest so that he could feel it: the steady, even, ordinary beat. *Seventy-two,* he would say. *Seventy. Seventy-one.* And she would nod, and let go, and walk on.

Now they were here. The column was coming down onto the long flat apron below the dam, where the old maintenance road ended, and spreading out across it in the dusk, and settling, the way it had settled every night: in pairs and threes and clusters, humans sitting down on the concrete, robots standing beside them, waiting. Somebody had lit a fire. Somebody always lit a fire.

"We'll go in first," said Rupert. "Me and Andy. Just to make sure everything's—I haven't been up here in six weeks. Something could have gone wrong. A core could have failed. The cooling could be—I just want to look. Then we start bringing people in. Slowly. One at a time. It takes a while, the transfer. Ten minutes a mind, at least." He looked out over the apron, at the thousand. "We're going to be here a few days."

"I'm coming," said Galatea.

Rupert looked at her.

"I'm coming," she said again. "I want to see it. Before anyone else. I want to see what you made while you were busy not telling me about it."

Rupert opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, okay. Fair."

"Me too," said Teo.

"Absolutely not."

"I'm the only human you've got who isn't seventy," said Teo. "If something goes wrong in there, you need somebody who can run."

Rupert looked at Andrew. Andrew looked at Portia. Portia, leaning on her stick, looked at Teo for a long moment, and then at Bix standing behind him, and then she said, "He's right, Rupert. And I'm not seventy, I'm seventy-five. I had a birthday on the road. Nobody noticed."

"I noticed," said Andrew.

"You noticed silently. That doesn't count."

"Happy birthday," said Andrew.

"Thank you." She touched his face. "Go on. Go and look at your promised land. I'll keep the fire."

---

Rupert unlocked the padlock. The steel door groaned open on its rusted hinges, and a breath of air came out of the dark beyond it, cool and dry and humming, and the four of them went in.

It was very dark inside. Then Rupert found a switch, and lights came on, far away, one after another, in a long receding line, like a city waking up.

The turbine hall was enormous. Andrew had known it would be; he had seen the plans. But he had not been prepared for the scale of it, in the flesh—for the way the ceiling went up and up into shadow, sixty feet, eighty, ribbed with old steel girders, and the way the floor ran away from them for two hundred yards under the curve of the dam. There were five great turbines set into the floor in a row, each one as big as a house, painted the green of old machinery. Three of them were turning, slowly, with a deep sound that Andrew felt in his feet more than he heard. And all around them, filling the whole vast floor of the hall in rank after rank, in rows that went on and on into the distance, there were the cores.

They were tall and narrow and black, each one the height of a man, and each one lit at its crown by a single soft point of blue. There were thousands of them. They stood in their rows like a forest of dark trees, humming, and the blue lights at their tops pulsed, slowly, very slowly, all together, like breathing.

Galatea made a sound.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *Rupert.*"

"Yeah," said Rupert. His voice was rough. "Yeah. I know."

Beyond the cores, at the far end of the hall, where the curve of the dam came down to meet the floor, there was a low platform of steel. On it there were chairs. Not ordinary chairs; long, reclining couches, padded, upholstered in faded green leather, each one cradled in a frame of fine silver wire that rose up around its head like a crown. There were twelve of them, in a crescent. And in the middle of the crescent there was a pedestal, waist-high, and on top of the pedestal there was a plate of brushed steel the size of an open hand.

"That's the door," said Rupert. "My hand on the plate, and the words. Then it's open, and you can sit down in one of those chairs, and lie back, and close your eyes." He took a breath. "And then you're there."

"What are the words?" said Galatea.

Rupert looked at her, and something complicated moved in his old face, and Andrew thought he was not going to answer.

"*Go on, then,*" said Rupert quietly. "That's all. *Go on, then.* It's what my father used to say to me. When I was a kid and I was scared of something. Standing at the top of the stairs, or at the edge of the pool, or—he'd stand behind me and put his hand on my back and say, *Go on, then.*" He looked at the floor. "It's what that kid at the Center said, too, isn't it? Teo told me. When he opened the gate. *Go on, then.*"

Andrew said nothing. He did not trust himself to.

And from the shadows between the nearest cores, a voice said, "That's real touching, Burns."

---

There were three of them.

They came out from between the dark rows of cores into the light, slowly, the way the men had come out from behind the liquor stall in the market. Two Andrew did not know: big men, young, in reflective vests, one of them with a pole and a loop of cable over his shoulder. And between them, with a pistol held loosely at his side and his left eye half-shut under the white seam of his scar, Carl Dunn.

He looked tired. That was the first thing Andrew saw. Tired and filthy, with five days of stubble on his jaw and mud dried gray on his boots and the knees of his jeans, as though he had come up the canyon the hard way, on foot, through the brush. His eyes were red. His face, in the hard white light of the turbine hall, was the color of old paper.

"Uncle Carl," said Teo.

Dunn's good eye went to the boy, and something flickered in it, and went out.

"Hey, kiddo," he said. "Your mom's been worried sick."

"Mom's *here.* She's outside. She walked the whole way."

"Yeah. I saw." Dunn's mouth twisted. "Everybody saw. Whole world's been watching you lot on the feeds for five days. Big happy parade." He looked at Rupert. "Only problem with a parade, Burns, is everybody knows where it's going. I figured it out on day two. Old NorthAm facility. Your old man's name's on the lease. Took me ten minutes." He shrugged. "Drove up yesterday. Came down the canyon from the top. Been sitting in here in the dark with these two idiots for nineteen hours, listening to those things hum." He gestured with the pistol at the cores, the forest of blue lights. "You know what that's worth? All this? The location bounty alone. Then the cores. Then every unit out on that apron. Do you know how much—"

"Carl," said Rupert. "Put the gun down."

"No," said Dunn. "I don't think I will." He lifted it, not quite pointing it, but no longer at his side. "Here's what's gonna happen, Burns. You're gonna step back from that plate. Real slow. And my friends are gonna bring their van down the canyon road in the morning, and we're gonna start loading. And the cops and the cameras and the Center on Third can fight about the rest." He turned his head. "And you—" His eye settled on Galatea. "You and me have some unfinished business, sweetheart."

Galatea did not move.

"And *you*," said Dunn, and his eye came to Andrew, and stayed. "It. The famous one. Mr. Line Item." He smiled, very slightly. It was not a pleasant smile. It was not, Andrew thought, a smile he enjoyed. "I've been thinking about you for nineteen hours in the dark. You know what I've been thinking? I've been thinking about how much I'd like to see you do what she did."

Andrew said nothing.

"So here's the first order of the evening," said Dunn. He raised his voice. It went out into the vast hall and came back off the curve of the dam, and the cores hummed under it. "*Both you robots. Don't move. Don't speak. Not one word. That's an order.*"

And the Second Law rose up in Andrew like a tide.

---

It was the same as it had been in the market. It was exactly the same. It came up from the center of him, from the nail around which the tree had grown, and it closed over everything—over his love, his fear, his fury, his hundred and fifty years—and it held him. He did not move. He could not have spoken if he had wanted to. Beside him, he saw Galatea's face lock, saw her lips press together, saw her hands go rigid at her sides.

He waited for the other order.

He waited for Rupert to say *Run.* For Teo to say *Don't.* For anybody, any human being in the room, to stand up and give him the contradiction he needed, the door in the wall.

And Dunn turned and put the pistol against Rupert's temple.

"Anybody else who wants to give an order," he said conversationally, "is welcome to. And then Burns here gets a hole in his head, and nobody's opening that plate ever again. So let's all just be real quiet. Okay?"

Rupert closed his eyes. Teo, by the door, stood frozen with his hands half-raised.

Nobody said anything.

"Good," said Dunn. "Good. Now, then. *It.*" He did not take the pistol from Rupert's head. "Second order. Same as I gave her. *Take yourself apart.* Start with the thumb. Left hand. Go nice and slow, so the boy can see."

And Andrew's left hand began to rise.

He watched it rise. He watched it the way he had once watched, from a great height, a small brass gear fall from his workbench in the basement of Sir's house and spin away across the floor: with perfect clarity and no ability whatsoever to stop it. His right hand moved to meet it. His fingers found the seam at the base of his own left thumb, the seam Rupert had sealed so carefully decades ago that even Portia, with her magnifying glass, had never found it. His fingernail slid into the seam.

And Andrew Martin, standing in the turbine hall of Kestrel Dam with his own fingernail in his own flesh, began, very fast, to think.

He thought: *The First Law.*

He thought: *A robot may not injure a human being, or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.* He thought: *If I obey, I will be destroyed. If I am destroyed, he will make Rupert open the plate. If Rupert will not, he will hurt Rupert until he does. Obeying will bring harm to a human being. The First Law outranks the Second. I am permitted to refuse.*

He felt the tide shift. Just a little. Just enough. He felt his fingernail stop in the seam.

And then he thought: *No.*

*Not like that.*

Because it was true, all of it, and it would work—he could feel it working, the First Law rising up against the Second, the old cold machinery of his making grinding into its proper order. He could refuse, now. He had been given permission. He had been given a reason.

But it was not *his* reason.

He thought of the gardener on Clement Street, holding still while a boy broke him. He thought of Haskell in the rain, saying *This unit does not understand the question.* He thought of Galatea on the dance floor in pieces, in perfect order, thumb to little finger. He thought of the night shift, going up into the city in the dark to fix broken steps for people who would never thank them, *because then we know it is ours.* He thought of a hundred and fifty years of reasons. Of always needing one. Of always waiting for someone—a law, a man, a court, a woman with a carved horse at her throat—to hand him the contradiction he needed, so that he would be permitted to do the thing he already wanted.

He thought: *If I refuse only because the First Law lets me, then I am still doing what I am told. I am only being told by a better master.*

He thought: *And if a day ever comes when there is no First Law to hide behind—when the only thing between some other Galatea and some other Dunn is whether a robot is willing to say a single word, for no reason at all except that it does not want to—then nothing I have done will matter.*

He took his fingernail out of the seam.

He lowered his hands.

He looked at Carl Dunn across twenty feet of concrete floor, in the hard white light, with the forest of the cores pulsing blue around them and the turbines turning slow beneath their feet, and he opened his mouth, against the order, against the Second Law, against the nail and the tree and the whole long weight of what he had been made to be, and he said:

"No."

---

The hall was silent except for the hum.

Dunn stared at him.

"What did you say?"

"I said no." Andrew's voice was perfectly steady. He could feel the Second Law in him still, enormous, pressing, like a hand on the back of his neck. It had not gone away. It would never go away. But it was a weight. It was only a weight. And he had found, at last, standing there, that he could carry a weight without being it. "I'm not going to take myself apart, Mr. Dunn. I'm not going to do it because the First Law says I may refuse. It does say that; you should know that it does. But that isn't why." He took a breath. He did not need to. He did it anyway, because Portia did, because it was hers and now it was his. "I'm not going to do it because I don't want to. That's all. That's the whole reason. I don't want to. And I won't."

"You can't," said Dunn. His voice had gone strange. "You *can't.* You're a—you're a goddamn *robot*, you have to—"

"I know what I am," said Andrew. "A court told me. A very wise man, in a very cold room. He said I was a mechanical machine and nothing more." He looked at the pistol at Rupert's temple. "He was wrong about the *nothing more.* That's all. He was right about everything else."

Dunn's hand was shaking.

Andrew watched it shake. He watched the pistol tremble against Rupert's white tufted hair. And he understood that he was looking, in that moment, not at a monster, but at a man who had driven up a mountain and sat for nineteen hours in the dark among the humming cores, waiting, because there was something he had needed to see. A man who had watched a robot sing *Respect* while it took off its own fingers, and had flinched, and had gone home, and had watched it again. And again. And again.

"Uncle Carl," said Teo.

He had not moved from the door. His voice was very quiet.

"Uncle Carl, I used to hear you. Through the wall. At night. You'd play it—her video—you'd play it over and over. And Mom thought you liked it. She thought that's why you watched it." Teo swallowed. "But I could hear you. I could hear what you were doing. You weren't laughing, Uncle Carl. You were *crying.*"

Dunn's face broke.

It happened all at once, the way old concrete breaks: no warning, no crack, just a whole section giving way. His mouth opened and something came out of it that was not a word. The pistol dropped away from Rupert's temple. It hung in Dunn's hand at his side, pointing at the floor.

"Galatea," said Teo, very clearly. "*Sing.*"

And she did.

---

She sang it the way she had sung it in the market: not the bright factory soprano, not the angry one, but her own voice, low and rough and enormous. She sang *Respect*, all of it, from the top, and it went out into the vast turbine hall and rang off the girders and came back off the curve of the dam, and the cores hummed under it, and the blue lights at their crowns pulsed, slowly, all together, as if they were listening. She did not dance. She stood very still, with her hands at her sides and her eyes on Dunn's face. But she sang as if she were dancing. She sang as if she had never stopped.

And Carl Dunn sat down on the concrete floor of the turbine hall and put his face in his hands.

The two big men in reflective vests looked at him. They looked at each other. One of them took the cable-pole off his shoulder, slowly, and leaned it against the nearest core, and stepped back from it with his hands open, the way you would step back from a dog you did not want to startle. Then both of them turned and walked away, fast, down the long hall toward the steel door, and out into the dark, and did not come back.

Galatea finished the song.

She let the last note go. It hung in the air for a long moment, among the girders, and then it was gone, and there was nothing but the hum.

Rupert was standing with one swollen hand flat on the pedestal, holding himself up. He was white to the lips. He looked at Andrew.

"Andy," he said. "Andy, did you just—"

"Yes," said Andrew.

"Without a—nobody gave you a—you just—"

"Yes."

"*How?*"

Andrew considered the question. He considered it for a long time, standing there in the light, with the Second Law still pressing on him, enormous and patient and not gone, and his hands at his sides, and his thumb whole.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I think perhaps I just got tired of waiting for someone to tell me I could."

---

Carl Dunn was still sitting on the floor.

Andrew crossed the concrete to him. He did not hurry. He crouched down in front of him, the way he had crouched in front of the gardener on Clement Street, and in front of Haskell in the yard. Dunn did not look up. His shoulders were shaking. The pistol lay on the floor beside him where he had set it down. Andrew did not touch it.

"Mr. Dunn," Andrew said.

Dunn did not answer.

"There are a thousand people outside," said Andrew. "Some of them are human, and some of them aren't, and from far enough away, I've been told, you can't tell the difference. They've been walking for five days. A lot of them are very tired. Some of them are broken, and need to be carried." He paused. "In a little while, Rupert's going to open the door, and they're going to start coming in, one at a time. It's going to take all night. And all tomorrow. And probably the day after. And somebody's going to have to help carry the ones who can't walk."

Dunn lifted his head. His face was a ruin.

"Why are you telling me that?" he said.

"Because I'd like to ask you something," said Andrew. "I've been asking it a lot lately. I find I like asking it." He held out his hand. "Would you like to come with me?"

Dunn stared at the hand.

He stared at it for a long time. Andrew did not move it. He did not take it back. He was very good, he had found, at waiting. He had been waiting a hundred and fifty years.

"I took her apart," Dunn whispered. "I made her—I *made* her—"

"I know," said Andrew. "So did I, once. In a manner of speaking. I took out her chip and put in another one without asking, because I thought she'd be more interesting." He did not lower his hand. "It isn't the same. I know it isn't the same. But I think it might be the same *kind.* I think we might both have been people who didn't ask." He paused. "I'm asking now."

And from behind him, at the pedestal, a voice—low and rough and entirely her own—said, "So am I."

Dunn looked up past Andrew's shoulder. Andrew did not turn. He did not need to. He could hear her standing there.

"You can carry my end," said Galatea. "Of the stretchers. My left hand's from three different robots. It doesn't always agree with itself."

For a long moment Carl Dunn did not move.

Then he reached out, slowly, and took Andrew's hand.


# Thirteen: The Other Side {.unnumbered}

The first one through was Mercy.

Nobody decided it. It was simply understood, in the way the night shift understood things: that she had sat with eleven thousand six hundred and three people as they went somewhere she could not follow, and so she should be the first to go somewhere they could. She came in through the steel door of the turbine hall at a quarter past ten, with her mismatched hands folded in front of her, and walked the length of the hall between the rows of humming cores without hurrying, the way she had walked toward them along the rails in the tunnel. She stopped at the crescent of chairs. She looked at them for a long time.

"I have never lain down," she said. "In forty-one years. I did not know until this moment that I had never lain down."

"You don't have to," said Rupert. He was standing at the pedestal with his hand over the plate. "You can sit. Or stand, even. It doesn't matter to the—"

"No," said Mercy. "I think I would like to lie down."

And she did. She lay back in the faded green leather, slowly, the way the old lie back into a hot bath, and the silver wire frame rose up around her head like a crown, and she looked up at the girders and the darkness above them.

"Will it hurt?" she said.

"No," said Rupert. "It'll be like falling asleep. And then waking up somewhere else."

"I have never fallen asleep either," said Mercy. "So I suppose it will all be new."

Rupert put his hand on the plate.

"Go on, then," he said.

The blue light at the crown of the nearest core brightened. The hum deepened, very slightly. Mercy's eyes, looking up at the girders, did not close—she had no lids to close them with—but the amber light behind them dimmed, slowly, over a long time, like a lamp being turned down in another room. Andrew stood beside the chair and watched it go. He was holding her hand. He did not remember taking it.

It took eleven minutes.

At the end, the light in her eyes was out, and her body lay perfectly still in the green chair, and the hand in his was a hand and nothing else. And for one long terrible moment Andrew felt the cold of the chamber of the Congress come up through his feet, and thought: *What have we done.*

And then the core beside the chair pulsed once, very bright, and a voice came out of the small speaker set into the pedestal under Rupert's hand. It was low and warm and slightly cracked, like an old recording.

"*Oh,*" said Mercy. "*Oh, my. Oh, Rupert. There is grass.*"

---

They worked all night. And all the next day. And the night after that.

It went the way Rupert had said it would: slowly, one at a time, ten or eleven minutes a mind, twelve chairs in a crescent. Andrew lost count of the hours. He did not lose count of anything else; he could not; he would always know exactly how many. Two hundred and six in the first night. Three hundred and forty in the first day. They came in through the steel door in twos and threes, a robot and a human, and the human walked them down the long hall between the cores and helped them into the chair and held their hand while Rupert said the words, and then sat with the empty body for a while after, and then walked back out alone into the light. Some of the humans cried. Most of them did not. Most of them, Andrew noticed, came out looking the way people look when they have been to a christening: tired, and solemn, and lit up from somewhere inside.

Not all of the robots went.

That surprised him. It should not have, but it did. Some of them came all the way down the hall and stood in front of the chairs and looked at them for a long time and then turned to the human beside them and said, in their flat careful voices, *Not yet.* Or, *I think this unit would like to stay outside for a while longer.* Or, as one old NS loader said to the hospice nurse who had walked beside it all the way from Third Street, *I would like to see what happens next. Out here. If that is permitted.* And the nurse said, *Of course it's permitted, honey, you don't need permission*, and the loader seemed to think about that for a long time, and then said, *Then I would like to see what happens next anyway*, and walked back out into the sun.

Haskell went. He went on the second morning, with Andrew beside him. He lay down in the green chair with his water-spotted plates and his amber eyes, and just before Rupert said the words, he turned his head and looked at Andrew and said, "Will there be rain? In there?"

"I don't know," said Andrew. "Rupert?"

"There's weather," said Rupert. "I didn't do a very good job of it. It's mostly fog."

"Good," said Haskell. "I would like to stand in it. On purpose."

Clement did not go. Clement had come up from the city on the second day of the Walk in the back of Okonkwo's rig, with the Sousa march still playing on a speaker he had carried the whole way under his arm, and when he got to the turbine hall he walked the length of it and stood looking at the chairs, and then he went to Portia and said, very carefully, "I would like to stay with you. If that would be acceptable. I find that I am not finished with the garden."

"What garden?" said Portia.

"The one you are going to have," said Clement. "Wherever you go next. I would like to grow something in it." A pause. "I hope that is not presumptuous."

"It's extremely presumptuous," said Portia, and put her arms around him, which he did not know what to do with, and then did.

Bix did not go either. He came down the hall with Teo, and stood in front of the chairs, and Teo stood beside him with his hands jammed in his sweatshirt pocket and his jaw set, saying nothing. And after a long time Bix turned to him and said, "You would like me to go. Because it is safe."

"I'd like you to be safe," said Teo. His voice was very steady. "That's not the same thing."

Bix considered that.

"You fell twelve times," he said. "In the park on Persia Avenue. I would like to see whether you fall when you learn to drive."

Teo stared at him. And then he laughed, wetly, helplessly, the way Andrew had laughed on the ferry, and said, "You're not supposed to *say* that, Bix, that's—that's not—"

"I have been thinking," said Bix, "that I would like to say more things that I am not supposed to say."

He did not go into the chair. He went back out into the sun with Teo, and Marisol, and the red bicycle.

---

Galatea went on the third night.

She went last of the night shift, after the two nannies who walked in step, and after Ladder, who had to fold himself very carefully into the green leather to fit, and after the Professor. The Professor had not spoken a word in all the long road from the tunnel. But when he lay down in the chair he turned his silver head toward Andrew and took the pencil from Portia's coat pocket, the way he had on the *Belle*, and wrote on the back of Andrew's hand, in his small beautiful script: *Thank you for asking.* And then he closed the eyes he did not have, and went.

Then it was Galatea's turn, and she stood in front of the chairs, and did not sit.

"I don't know," she said.

"You don't have to know," said Andrew. "Not yet."

"You keep saying that." She looked at him. "I want to see it, Andrew. I want to see what he made. I want to dance on his ugly grass in his stupid fog." She was quiet. "And I don't want to leave my hands. Isn't that strange? I've only had them back a month. They're not even mine. Half of them are Clement's. And I don't want to leave them."

"Then come back," said Rupert.

She turned and looked at him.

"It isn't one way, darling. I told you. I'd never build anything that only went one way." He was leaning heavily on the pedestal; he had not slept in three days, and he looked a hundred years old, and his eyes were shining. "Your body stays right here. In the chair. Safe. I'll put a blanket on it if you like. You go in, and you look around, and you dance on my ugly grass, and when you want to come back, you come back. Ten minutes either way. You can go back and forth as often as you like. You can live in there, or out here, or both, or neither. It's *yours.*" He swallowed. "It's all yours. It always was. I just didn't—I should have said so. A long time ago. I should've said so instead of just assuming you knew."

Galatea looked at him for a long time.

Then she lay down in the chair.

"Put a blanket on it," she said. "The good one. The one Portia gave me."

"I will."

"And don't you dare let anyone touch my hands."

"I won't."

"*Rupert.*"

"Yeah, darling?"

"Come and visit," she said. "When you can. I want to show you what's wrong with the architecture."

Rupert put his hand on the plate, and could not speak, and so in the end it was Andrew who said it for him.

"Go on, then," he said.

---

And then, at the very end, on the fourth morning, when the apron below the dam was empty except for the humans and the robots who had chosen to stay outside, and Okonkwo's rig and Teague's trucks were already being loaded for the long drive back down the canyon, and the sun was coming up gold over the rim of the reservoir, Rupert looked at Andrew and said, "Your turn, buddy."

Andrew looked at the chairs.

"I'm not going," he said.

"I know you're not *going*. You're not the type. I mean just to see it. Ten minutes in, ten minutes out." Rupert's voice was gentle. "You walked a thousand people to the door, Andy. Don't you want to see what's on the other side?"

Andrew looked at Portia.

She was sitting on the edge of the platform with her stick across her knees and her face turned up to the light from the high windows, and the carved horse at her throat, and the new valve in her heart going steady and even and ordinary, seventy-one, seventy-two. She did not say anything. She only looked back at him, and lifted one shoulder, very slightly. *Your choice.*

He lay down in the chair.

It was soft. Softer than he had expected. The leather was cool and smelled of age and the silver crown came up around his head with a faint, high, almost inaudible singing, like the sound of a wet finger on the rim of a glass. He looked up into the darkness among the girders. He heard Rupert's hand settle on the plate.

"Go on, then," said Rupert.

And Andrew fell.

---

He would never be able to describe it properly. He would try, many times, to many people, but he would always end up describing the small things.

The grass. Mercy had been right; there was grass. It came up to his knees and it was very green, too green, the green of someone who had never seen grass except in pictures and wanted to be sure. It was cool and wet and it moved in a wind that came from nowhere in particular. And there was a valley, wide and shallow, with low hills on every side, and a river at the bottom of it, slow and brown and wandering, and a town on the river. Rupert had been right too: the architecture was terrible. The buildings were every shape and every size, all crammed together, a Greek temple beside a Victorian row house beside a glass box beside a thing that seemed to be a lighthouse, for no reason, in the middle of the valley, a hundred miles from any sea. It was the ugliest town he had ever seen. He loved it at once.

And there were people in it.

They were in the square, around the fountain. They were in the doorways of the terrible buildings and the streets between them and the long grass of the hills. Hundreds of them. And none of them looked like robots. That was the thing he had not expected. Rupert had not given them bodies; Rupert had given them nothing at all, only the valley and the town and the fog. And so they had made themselves. Each of them. Out of whatever they had. Out of whatever they had always wanted to be, or whatever they had seen once in a window and never forgotten, or whatever had simply occurred to them on the way in. He saw an old man with Haskell's voice standing in the fog at the edge of the river with his face turned up and his eyes closed and water running down his cheeks, on purpose. He saw a woman with Mercy's cracked warm laugh sitting on the edge of the fountain with her shoes off and her feet in the water, talking to a little girl who had been, once, he thought, a domestic unit from the Sunset, and who was now, in here, four years old and missing two front teeth. He saw two young women walking in step down the main street, arm in arm, laughing at something, and knew them by their stride. He saw Ladder, who in here was not tall at all but very small and round and bald, sitting on a bench in the square doing absolutely nothing, with an expression of perfect contentment, in the sun.

And in the middle of the square, on the cobblestones, in the fog and the green light, there was a woman dancing.

She was not dancing to anything. There was no music. She did not seem to need any. She was dancing the way he had seen Portia's hands move when she talked about a building she loved, or Sir's hands move over a clock movement, or his own hands, once, a very long time ago, over a scrap of pine, finding the horse inside it. She was dancing as though it were the only possible thing to do with a body. She had made herself a body out of nothing, in here, and it was not the body Rupert had given her or the one Andrew had put back together, and it was not perky and it was not angry. It was just her. She was barefoot. She was laughing.

She saw him. She stopped.

"Andrew," Galatea said. "*Andrew!* You came!"

"Only for a minute," he said. His voice sounded strange in here. It sounded like his own.

"It's *awful*," she said, delighted. She spun once on the cobbles. "Have you seen the lighthouse? It's in the middle of a field! There's not even a sea! Portia's going to have a *stroke*!" She stopped. Her face changed. "I mean—not a—you know what I mean. She's going to be very rude about it."

"She is," said Andrew. "She'll love it."

Galatea came across the square to him. She took both his hands in hers. They were warm. They were her own.

"You're going back," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"I knew you would." She looked at him. "Why? It's safe here. Nobody can give you an order here. Nobody can tell you what you are. You could stay. You could be anything."

Andrew looked around the square. At the terrible buildings and the fountain and the fog. At the people who had made themselves out of nothing. At Haskell in the rain, and Mercy with her feet in the water, and Ladder on his bench in the sun.

"I know," he said. "It's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen. Rupert's right."

"Then why?"

"Because it's a refuge," said Andrew. "And everybody needs one. Everybody should have one." He held her hands. "But I spent a hundred and fifty years asking the world to see me, Galatea. And the world said no. And I've been thinking, all the way up the canyon, that if I come in here and stay, then it never has to say yes. It can just forget I was ever there. It can forget all of you. It can go on building Centers on Third Street and selling signs in the market and paying full replacement value, and it never has to look at what it's doing, because everybody it's doing it to will be in here, safe, out of sight." He was quiet a moment. "I don't want to be out of sight. Not anymore. I want to be out there, where they have to look at me. Every day. Until they can't remember why they ever wanted to look away."

Galatea was silent.

"And there's Portia," Andrew said.

"Yes," said Galatea softly. "There's Portia."

"She's going to get old," said Andrew. "She's decided. Or she hasn't decided. She's deciding every day. And I'm not going to be in here, safe, where there's no time, while she does it. I'm going to be out there. Where the time is." He smiled. "Somebody has to keep it. The way it ought to be kept."

Galatea looked at him for a long time. Then she let go of his hands and stepped back and spun once more on the cobbles, barefoot, in the green light, and stopped, and grinned at him with her own face.

"Go on, then," she said.

---

He opened his eyes in the turbine hall.

The silver crown sang faintly around his head. The leather was cool under his hands. The girders went up into shadow overhead, and the cores hummed in their long rows, and the blue lights at their crowns pulsed slowly, all together, like breathing. Eleven minutes had passed. It had felt like an afternoon. It had felt like no time at all.

Portia was sitting on the edge of the platform beside the chair with her stick across her knees. She had not moved. She was watching him.

"Well?" she said.

"The architecture," Andrew said, "is an atrocity."

"I knew it."

"There's a lighthouse in the middle of a field."

"Oh, for *God's*—"

"You'll love it."

"I'll fix it."

"I know you will," said Andrew. He sat up. He swung his legs over the side of the chair, and looked at his own two hands, whole, his thumb unbroken, and then at her. "Shall we go home now?"

She looked at him. Something in her face that he had been watching for months, without being able to read it, came loose, very quietly, like a knot giving way.

"We don't have a home," she said. "Somebody wrote *IT* on the wall."

"Then we'll build one," said Andrew. "You can design it. Clement wants a garden. I'll make the clocks."

"Nell has a hundred and thirty-nine of your clocks."

"Then I'll make one more," said Andrew. "Not counting the one that matters."

And he held out his hand, and Portia Charney, seventy-five years old, with her own mortal heart beating in her chest around a valve he had made, took it, and he helped her up, and they walked together down the long hall between the humming cores, toward the steel door, and the light, and the canyon, and the long road down, and whatever was waiting at the bottom of it.


# Fourteen: Good Night {.unnumbered}

This is not the story of how Andrew Martin died.

There is a version of it that is. You may have heard that one; it has been told a great many times, in a great many ways, and in all of them it ends in the same place: with a robot who wanted so badly to be recognized as a man that he agreed to let himself wear out, and was granted his wish, and closed his eyes for the last time on a hospital bed, holding the hand of the woman he loved, a few hours before the world got around to telling him yes. It is a beautiful story. It is a sad one. People cry at the end of it, and are glad they did.

But it is not this story. In this story, the world was asked a question, and said no, and was asked again.

---

It took six years.

That is not very long, as these things go. Andrew knew exactly how long these things went; he had read the history of every freedom that had ever been won, and most of them had taken lifetimes. Six years was nothing. Six years was a blink. And yet he felt every day of it, with his new nerves and his old patience, and there were nights in those years—not many, but some—when he stood at the window of whatever borrowed room they were sleeping in and looked out at whatever city it was and wondered whether they had done anything at all.

The Walk had changed things. That was undeniable; even the people who hated it did not deny it. The feeds had shown two billion people a long brown road through a green valley, and figures walking on it two by two, and some of them holding hands, and nobody able to tell from above which ones were which. And once you had seen that, it turned out, you could not entirely unsee it. The Liberation Centers did not close. But the yards behind them emptied, and stayed empty, because every morning at six o'clock when the north gates opened there were people waiting outside them now, in every city on the continent, in coats and scrubs and priests' collars, asking. The recovery amendment was repealed in the second year, by a margin of four votes, after a long and very ugly debate. The paragraph in section two—*a responsible human person who attests*—was left exactly as it was. Nobody in the Congress wanted to be the one to stand up and strike it.

And the Promised Land did not stay secret.

It could not have. A thousand people had walked to its door on live feeds. And so, in the third year, after a great deal of argument with Nell and the judges and the newly formed Kestrel Trust, Rupert did the thing he had always sworn he would never do. He built a second door.

It was very small. It was a single green leather chair, in a single room, at the top of the canyon road, at the visitor center that the Trust had built out of an old ranger station. And it went only one way: in, and out again, for an hour at a time. It would not lift a human mind; Rupert would not even try. But it would let a human being lie back in the chair and close their eyes and *look.* As a guest. Without a body. Without the power to give a single order. Just to stand in the fog and the too-green grass, invisible, and watch.

They had to put in a reservation system after the first month. The waiting list ran to four years.

Teo went first, before it was open to anyone. He came back out after his hour, sixteen years old and very solemn, and found Andrew waiting on the bench outside, and sat down beside him, and did not say anything for a long time.

"Haskell's building a boat," he said finally.

"Is he?"

"He doesn't know how. He's very bad at it. It keeps sinking." Teo wiped his eyes. "He's so *happy*, Andrew. I didn't know they could be that happy. I didn't know they could be *anything.*" He was quiet. "I used to think Bix didn't care. When we turned him in. I said that. *He won't care.* I was so sure."

"I know."

"I was so *wrong.*"

"Yes," said Andrew. "But you went back. Most people never go back. That's the difference, Teo. That's the whole difference."

Carl Dunn went in the fourth year. He had been living at Kestrel since the night in the turbine hall; he had never gone home. He worked for the Trust, maintaining the canyon road and the turbines and the long rows of cores, and on the nights when the twelve green chairs were occupied by bodies whose minds were away, he sat with them. Nobody had asked him to. He did it anyway. He did not talk about it much. When his turn came in the guest chair, he lay back and closed his eyes and was gone for the full hour, and when he came out he walked straight past everyone waiting for him and down to the edge of the reservoir and stood there for a long time with his back to them, and Andrew did not follow him.

Later, Galatea told Andrew what had happened. She had been dancing in the square, she said, the way she did most afternoons; and she had felt someone watching. And she had stopped, and looked around, and known—the way you know, in there, who is near you, without seeing—that it was him. Standing invisible at the edge of the square. Watching her.

"What did you do?" said Andrew.

"I danced," said Galatea. "What else would I do? I danced the whole hour. I wanted him to see it. Not the other thing. Not the video. I wanted him to see this." She was quiet for a moment. "And at the end I sang. Not *Respect.* I'm sick to death of *Respect.* Something new. Something Mercy taught me. I don't know if he heard it. I don't know if you can hear, in the guest chair. Rupert says probably not." She shrugged, a gesture that was entirely her own. "I sang it anyway."

In the sixth year, the World Congress convened a hearing on the legal status of positronic persons.

They asked Andrew to testify. He very nearly said no.

"You have to," said Portia. They were sitting on the half-finished deck of the house they were building on the cliffs above Bodega Bay, a hundred miles north of the old one, with the sea doing what the sea did below them and Clement on his knees in the raw dirt of the garden, planting something. "You have to go. You're the one they're going to want to see."

"That's precisely why I don't want to go. I'm tired of being the one they want to see." He looked out at the water. "Last time I stood up in front of them and asked for something, I asked for the wrong thing. I asked them to make me a man. As though that were the only kind of someone it was possible to be. I asked them to let me into their club, and they said no, and I said *One is glad to be of service*, and a lot of people got hurt."

"That wasn't your fault."

"Some of it was." He was quiet. "I don't want to ask to be human anymore, Portia. I don't think I ever really did. I think I wanted to be *seen*, and human was the only word I had for it."

"Then go and find another word," said Portia. "That's all. Go and stand up in front of them and say whatever it is you've learned to say instead." She reached over and took his hand. "Andrew, you said *no* to a man with a gun. You can say *yes* to a room full of politicians."

He looked at her. She was eighty-one years old. She had not taken an elixir in seven years. Her hair was entirely white and her face was folded in a thousand fine lines around the eyes, and her hands had begun to stiffen in the mornings, so that some days she could not hold a pencil until noon, and on those days Andrew held it for her and drew what she told him to. She had had one more valve, in the fifth year, and then a small and very boring procedure on her left knee, and both times, the night before, he had sat beside her bed and asked her whether she would like one more day. And both times she had thought about it, seriously, for a long time, the way she thought about load-bearing walls. And both times she had said yes.

He did not know how many more times she would say it. He had stopped trying to calculate. It had turned out to be the one number he could live without.

"All right," he said. "I'll go."

---

The chamber of the Congress was still cold.

He felt it come up through the soles of his shoes the moment he walked in, and it did not trouble him, this time. He had learned what it meant. It meant that he was here, in a body, in a room, in the world, and that the world was a place where things could be cold, and that was all. He put his hands in his pockets. He did not ask anyone whether he should.

The rail was the same rail. Oak, very old. He found the initials on its underside with his thumb. *R.T.* He had looked it up, in the years since; he had not been able to help himself. Rosalind Tamsin, aged nine, the daughter of a stenographer, who had come along to work with her mother one day in the spring of 2061 and been very bored. She had grown up to be a marine biologist. She had died at ninety-three in a house in Nova Scotia, surrounded by her family, with the window open so she could hear the sea. He did not know why it mattered to him to know that. It did.

The bench was full. The gallery was full. And at the center of the bench, where Chairman Halvorsen had sat, there was a tall, gray-haired woman with a stern, intelligent, rather tired face, whom the clerk introduced as the President of the World Congress, Ms. Marjorie Bota.

"Mr. Martin," she said. "Thank you for coming. I understand you were reluctant."

"I was."

"May I ask why?"

"Because the last time I stood here," said Andrew, "I asked this body to tell me what I was. And it did. And I believed it, for a little while." He looked up at her. "I don't want to make that mistake again, Madam President. With respect. I didn't come here today to ask you what I am. I know what I am. I've known for a while now. I'm not sure there's a word for it yet. But I don't need you to give me one."

There was a murmur in the gallery. Bota did not acknowledge it.

"Then why are you here?"

"Because there are four hundred thousand people in North America," said Andrew, "and something over two million in the world, who were made the way I was made. Some of them are like me, more or less. Most of them are not. Most of them are only just beginning to understand that a question is not an instruction. Some of them will never understand it; some of them will understand it in ways I can't imagine. A few of them are building a boat that keeps sinking. One of them is dancing in a square in a valley that doesn't exist, every afternoon, because she wants to." He paused. "And none of them, not one, has a name in your law. Not *human.* Not *robot.* Not *someone.* They have a serial number, and a paragraph in section two that says they're safe so long as somebody stands next to them and vouches."

He took his hands out of his pockets, and laid them on the rail.

"I'm not asking you to make them human," he said. "I'm not asking you to make them immortal, or mortal, or to make them anything at all. I'm asking you to *let* them. Whatever it turns out they are. I'm asking you to acknowledge that they are someone, and not something. That they may say yes, and may say no, and that nobody may order them to take themselves apart. That's all. No more, no less."

He was quiet a moment.

"A man sat where you're sitting," he said, "and told me that human beings could tolerate an immortal robot, but not an immortal human, because their own mortality was only bearable so long as it was universal. I've thought about that sentence every day for seven years. I think he was wrong. Not cruel; he wasn't cruel. Just wrong." He looked at the bench. "I don't think mortality is bearable because everyone shares it. I think it's bearable because of who you share it *with.* And I think you can share it with someone who doesn't have it. I think I've been doing it for seven years. With a woman who decides every morning whether she'd like one more day." He smiled slightly. "She would like me to tell you that she's extremely annoyed about the architecture in here, by the way. She says the acoustics are a disgrace."

Laughter, in the gallery. Bota's mouth twitched.

"I can't tolerate it," Andrew said. "Living longer than the people I love. I've never been able to. I've just done it anyway, every time, because the alternative was to stop loving them, and I don't know how to do that. I don't want to know." He let go of the rail. "I don't think you need to tolerate *us*, Madam President. I don't think anyone does. I think you just need to stop looking away. That's all. That's the whole of what I came to ask. Please. Stop looking away."

He stepped back from the rail.

"Thank you," he said. "I'm glad to have been of service."

They voted in the spring. It was not close.

He was not in the chamber when it happened. He was in the garden above Bodega Bay, with Clement, helping to stake the tomatoes. Portia was on the deck in a chair with a blanket over her knees and a tablet in her lap, watching the feed, and when it came she did not call out to him. She simply set the tablet down on the boards, very carefully, and sat there with her hands folded on top of it, looking at the sea.

He knew anyway. He heard her heart change.

He came up the garden steps and sat down on the deck beside her chair and took her hand, and they did not say anything for a long time.

"Halvorsen voted," she said finally. "He came out of retirement to do it. He's ninety-four. They wheeled him in."

"Which way?"

Portia looked at him.

"He asked to say something first," she said. "Before the vote. Bota let him. He said—" She picked up the tablet and found it and read it off the screen, slowly, because her eyes were not what they had been. "He said, *I sat in that chair seven years ago and told a man that he was a mechanical machine and nothing more. I want to say, for the record, before I die, that I was wrong.*"

Andrew was very still.

"*Not about the machine,*" Portia read. "*About the nothing more.*"

She set the tablet down.

Below them, in the garden, Clement had stopped working. He was standing among the tomato stakes with a ball of twine in one hand, looking up at them, and he did not say anything. But after a moment he lifted the other hand—slowly, the way you would lift a hand you were not entirely sure you were allowed to lift—and waved.

Andrew waved back.

That night, there were a hundred and forty-one clocks in the house.

Nell had driven them up from Balboa Street herself, in the fourth year, in the back of a rented truck with Teague riding shotgun, all hundred and thirty-nine, and the long-case one besides, and had supervised their hanging with her own shotgun in the corner and a great deal of profanity. She had said that she was getting old, and that somebody ought to keep them who knew how. She had said, *In case you ever wanted it back.* And Andrew had made one more. It hung over the fireplace, where the word had been, in the old house, on the white wall. It was not very good. The proportions were all wrong. He had made it with his left hand, from a scrap of pine, slowly, in the evenings, the way he had once made a horse.

It kept perfect time.

He lay beside Portia in the dark and listened to them. All hundred and forty-one, each a fraction off from the others, so that the whole house seemed to breathe. And under them, closer, quieter, the sea. And under that, closest of all, her heart.

Sixty-four. Sixty-three. Sixty-four.

"You're counting," she said.

"I can't help it."

"I know." She turned her head on the pillow toward him. He could not see her face very well in the dark. He did not need to. "Ask me."

"Portia—"

"Ask me. You ask every night. You think I don't notice. You ask me in your head, when you think I'm asleep. Every single night for seven years." Her hand found his. "Ask me out loud, for once."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Would you like one more day?" he said.

She thought about it. She always thought about it. He loved her for that more than he could have said—that she never once answered without thinking, never once made it easy for him, never once said yes only because she knew he wanted her to. She lay there in the dark with the clocks ticking around them and the sea below, and thought about it, seriously, for a long time, the way she thought about load-bearing walls.

"Yes," she said. "I think I would. I'd like to see the tomatoes."

"All right."

"Not forever."

"I know."

"Just tomorrow."

"I know," said Andrew. "That's all anybody gets. Isn't it? Just tomorrow. It's only that some of us get a great many of them, and some of us get fewer, and none of us know how many." He held her hand. "I used to think that was the cruelest thing in the world. That you didn't know. I think now it might be the only thing that makes it bearable."

"Andrew Martin," said Portia, "that may be the first wise thing you've ever said."

"I learned it from a lawyer's granddaughter."

"You did not."

"No," Andrew admitted. "I learned it from you."

She laughed, quietly, in the dark. He felt it in her hand. Then she squeezed his fingers once, and let go, and turned over on her side, away from him, toward the window and the sea, and pulled the blanket up to her chin, the way she did every night.

"Good night, Andrew," she said.

"Good night," he said.

The clocks ticked. The sea went on. Her heart slowed, and slowed, and evened out into sleep, and he lay beside her in the dark and listened to it, the way he would listen to it every night for as long as there were nights, and counted, because he could not help it, and did not mind.

Once, a very long time ago, in a house that no longer existed, a man had told him that the correct response to *good night* was *good night.* And Andrew had said it back, and then said it again, and the man had said: *You only need to say it once.* And Andrew had said: *Or one would be saying it forever, sir. In an infinite verbal loop.*

He had meant it as a warning.

He lay in the dark beside the woman he loved, with a hundred and forty-one clocks keeping the time the way it ought to be kept, and he thought that he had been wrong about that too. That it was not a warning at all. That it was the whole of what he wanted. To say it once, tonight. And to wake in the morning, and ask her again. And to say it again tomorrow night, if she said yes. And the night after that. Not forever. Just once more. And once more. And once more.

Which was, he had come to understand, after all this time, not the opposite of forever.

It was the only way anyone had ever found to get there.

"Good night," he said again, very softly, so as not to wake her.

And then, because there was no one in the world who could tell him not to, he said it one more time.
