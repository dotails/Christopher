# Research & Craft Notes

## Sources studied
- **Your outline** (*Bicentennial Man Alternate Ending*): the beats that form the spine of the book. The ruling denies Andrew his humanity but recognizes his life. Robot sales are halted. A Liberation incentive backfires into violence. Andrew and Portia's home is ransacked. Galatea is found in pieces and rebuilt over a month of night scavenging. The Promised Land is a virtual sanctuary only Rupert can open. The book follows the journey through the city, the allies, the clues, the safe house, the reunion, and the decision. The book then carries it through to the exodus, the gate and the ending.
- **The film script** (Nicholas Kazan, 1999): voices, running jokes and lines that come back as motifs. These include "One is glad to be of service", "good night… or one would be saying it forever", the chicken crossing the road, "Now jump", Lloyd's "It", Woofy, the carved horse, the clocks, "a thing is itself… what about ice? what about steam?", "do the wrong thing", Rupert's nose, Galatea's two personality chips and "Respect", the flower seller's "take as many as you need", Bill Feingold's clock, and the 5:15 p.m. switch-on time.
- **Isaac Asimov, "The Bicentennial Man" (1976, Hugo-winning novelette)** and **Asimov & Robert Silverberg, *The Positronic Man* (1992)**:
  - In the novelette, two men threaten to make Andrew take himself apart and he is legally bound to let them. That is the core horror here: the Second Law used as a weapon.
  - The Chairman's ruling is built on the novelette's line that human beings can tolerate an immortal robot but not an immortal human.
  - Andrew's prosthetic inventions living inside the people who judge him. That becomes the Chairman's kidney and the checkpoint sergeant's mother's heart valves.
  - Andrew's patient campaign for robot rights through the law.
- **Asimov's "Frankenstein complex"**: his name for humanity's fear of its own creations, which he spent his career writing against. The mobs, the bounty hunting and the word "IT" all come from this.
- **Critical reading of the original** (e.g. Andrew Liptak's essay on the story): critics often say Asimov's ending implies that life matters *because it ends*, and that Andrew earns personhood by giving up his immortality. This book argues the opposite, which is your objection to the film. Recognition shouldn't have to be paid for with a death. "Mortality is bearable not because everyone shares it, but because of who you share it *with*."

## Craft choices
- **Close third person with free indirect style**, so the narration takes on each character's mind: Andrew's counting and precision, Portia's eye for buildings, Galatea's broken-up reawakening. The point of view moves only at chapter breaks.
- **Restraint over melodrama.** The worst moments (Galatea laid out in perfect order, the yard behind the Center) are described plainly and left for the reader to feel. Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* and *Never Let Me Go* are the main models for writing artificial minds this way.
- **Setups and payoffs.** Nearly every image comes back changed later:
  - "One is glad to be of service" goes from defeat to a choice the night shift makes freely, and finally to a farewell.
  - Haskell's "does not understand the question" is answered in the yard.
  - The chicken joke finally lands on the ferry.
  - The clocks smashed in Chapter 2 return as 141 in the last chapter.
  - "Good night" is turned into the book's last argument.
- **A rule that drives the plot.** Asimov's Three Laws are applied strictly. That gives the story its danger (any human can order a robot to destroy itself), its tactic (conflicting orders force a robot to choose) and its climax (Andrew refuses with no law permitting it). The climax shows he is a person without killing him.
- **Antagonists who can change.** The book has no cartoon villain. Dunn's cruelty comes from losing his job and from guilt, and he is redeemed through carrying stretchers and sitting watch.
- **No one dies on the page.** Portia's mortality is still the emotional heart of the book. It is settled the way your outline points: she says yes one day at a time and is never forced.
